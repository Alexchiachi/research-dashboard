\[EN\] The Sleepy Girl Mocktail and the Quiet Revolution of Functional Drinks  
────────────────────────────

Introduction

This week, one of the internet's most persistent food stories was not a new restaurant or a celebrity chef, but a drink you make in your own kitchen. The "Sleepy Girl Mocktail" — tart cherry juice, magnesium powder, and sparkling water — kept climbing the trend charts alongside a broader wave of "functional mocktails," alcohol-free drinks engineered to do something: help you sleep, calm your nerves, or steady your gut. Tart cherry juice alone posted double-digit year-over-year growth, and the mocktail category has become one of the biggest engines of food virality on TikTok. What looks like a passing wellness fad is, on closer inspection, a grassroots redesign of how a generation drinks, socializes, and cares for itself.

The Social Innovation Angle

Social innovation is best understood as a new solution to a social need that is more effective, sustainable, or just than existing options — and that shifts who holds power in the system. The functional mocktail movement qualifies on three levels.

First, it reframes a public-health problem as a lifestyle upgrade rather than a lecture. Decades of top-down anti-alcohol messaging produced guilt but little behavior change. The mocktail trend achieves what campaigns could not: it makes not-drinking aspirational, photogenic, and social. The innovation is not the ingredient list; it is the cultural repackaging. By attaching sleep, calm, and beauty to abstention, creators converted a deprivation ("no alcohol") into an acquisition ("better rest"). That reframing is a classic social-innovation move — changing the story so the healthier choice becomes the default desirable one.

Second, it democratizes wellness. The recipe is cheap, open-source, and endlessly remixable. Nobody owns it; there is no gatekeeping clinic or premium subscription. A teenager, a shift worker, and a retiree can all participate with pantry staples. This is distributed innovation — value created and improved by a crowd rather than a corporation — and it lowers the barrier to self-care that formal wellness industries tend to raise.

Third, it is quietly building new social infrastructure. The "sober-curious" movement that these drinks feed is producing alcohol-free bars, dry events, and workplace norms where a mocktail is an unremarkable choice. That matters for inclusion: people in recovery, pregnant people, those on medication, observant communities, and anyone who simply feels better without alcohol gain a socially legitimate way to be present. When a social ritual (the shared drink) is redesigned so more people can join without stigma, that is social innovation in its truest sense — expanding participation.

There are risks worth naming. Commercialization can hollow out the movement: pre-bottled "functional" drinks with unproven claims and premium prices re-erect the very barriers the DIY version tore down. Some ingredient claims outrun the evidence, and a wellness aesthetic can tip into anxiety-driven consumption. The social-innovation test is whether the movement keeps power with ordinary people or hands it back to brands.

Actionable Takeaways

\- For community organizations and public-health teams: stop moralizing and start co-creating. Partner with local creators to make the healthy option desirable rather than dutiful. A recipe card beats a warning label.  
\- For hospitality and small businesses: build a genuine alcohol-free menu, not a token one. Price mocktails fairly; an inclusive drinks list is now a competitive advantage and a signal of who you welcome.  
\- For workplaces and event organizers: normalize the mocktail as a default, not a special request, so no one has to explain why they are not drinking.  
\- For consumers: treat the DIY, open-source version as the point. Be skeptical of premium "functional" claims and keep the innovation in your own kitchen, where it stays affordable and honest.

The lasting lesson is that the most powerful health interventions of this decade may not come from clinics or regulators, but from ordinary people redesigning a shared ritual — and making the better choice the more attractive one.

\[繁中\] 「助眠女孩特調」與無酒精機能飲品的社交革命  
────────────────────────────

引言

本週互聯網上最有黏著度的美食話題，主角既不是新開的餐廳，也不是名廚，而是一杯你在自家廚房就能調出來的飲料。「助眠女孩特調（Sleepy Girl Mocktail）」——酸櫻桃汁、鎂粉、加上氣泡水——持續在趨勢榜上攀升，並帶動更大一波「機能無酒精特調（functional mocktails）」熱潮：這些不含酒精的飲品被刻意設計來「做一件事」，例如幫助入睡、安撫情緒、或調理腸胃。光是酸櫻桃汁一項，年增率就達到兩位數，而無酒精特調更成為 TikTok 上最強的美食流量引擎之一。乍看之下這只是又一波養生潮流，細看卻是一整個世代對於「如何飲用、如何社交、如何照顧自己」的草根式重新設計。

社會創新角度分析

社會創新的核心，是針對某個社會需求，提出比既有方案「更有效、更永續、或更公平」的新解法，並且改變了系統中的權力分配。機能特調運動在三個層面上都符合這個定義。

第一，它把一個公共衛生問題，重新包裝成一種「生活升級」，而不是說教。過去數十年由上而下的反酗酒宣導，製造了大量罪惡感，卻很少真正改變行為。特調熱潮做到了宣導做不到的事：它讓「不喝酒」變得令人嚮往、上相、且具有社交價值。真正的創新不在那份食譜，而在文化上的重新包裝。當創作者把睡眠、平靜與美麗，連結到「戒酒」這件事上，就把一種「剝奪感」（不能喝酒）轉化成一種「獲得感」（睡得更好）。這種「換一個故事說」的手法，正是典型的社會創新——改寫敘事，讓較健康的選項自然而然成為眾人渴望的預設值。

第二，它讓「養生」變得平民化。這份配方便宜、開源、可無限改編。沒有人擁有它，沒有把關的診所、也沒有高價訂閱制。青少年、輪班工人、退休長者，都能用家中廚櫃裡的常備品參與其中。這是一種「分散式創新」——價值由群眾而非企業創造與改良——它降低了自我照顧的門檻，而正規養生產業往往反其道而行、把門檻抬高。

第三，它正悄悄建立新的社會基礎設施。這些飲品所餵養的「清醒好奇（sober-curious）」運動，正催生出無酒精酒吧、無酒精聚會，以及「點一杯特調毫不突兀」的職場文化。這對「共融」意義重大：戒癮者、孕婦、服藥者、有信仰戒律的社群，以及任何單純覺得不喝酒比較舒服的人，都因此獲得了一種在社交場合被正當接納的存在方式。當一項社交儀式（共飲）被重新設計，讓更多人能夠沒有污名地參與，這就是最本質的社會創新——擴大參與。

當然也有必須點名的風險。商業化可能掏空這場運動：那些預先瓶裝、宣稱「機能」卻缺乏實證、又賣高價的飲品，很可能重新築起 DIY 版本原本拆掉的那道牆。部分成分的功效宣稱跑在證據之前，而養生美學也可能滑向由焦慮驅動的消費。檢驗它是不是真社會創新的標準，就是：這場運動究竟把權力留在一般人手上，還是又交還給了品牌。

可行的啟示與行動建議

\- 給社區組織與公衛團隊：別再說教，改為「共創」。與在地創作者合作，把健康選項變得令人嚮往，而非義務。一張食譜卡的效果，勝過一張警告標籤。  
\- 給餐飲與小型店家：認真做一份無酒精菜單，而非聊備一格。把特調合理定價；一份共融的飲品單，如今是競爭優勢，也是「你歡迎誰」的訊號。  
\- 給職場與活動主辦方：把「點特調」正常化為預設選項，而非特殊要求，讓沒有人需要解釋自己為何不喝酒。  
\- 給消費者：DIY、開源版本才是重點。對高價「機能」宣稱保持懷疑，把創新留在自家廚房裡——在那裡，它才會維持平價與誠實。

真正的啟示是：這個十年最有力的健康介入，也許不來自診所或監管單位，而來自一群平凡人重新設計了一項共同儀式——並且，把更好的選擇變成更迷人的選擇。

\[EN\] Viral Thrift Hauls and the Crowd-Powered Circular Economy  
────────────────────────────

Introduction

This week the fashion internet was ruled not by a runway but by a rummage. Viral thrift hauls, "thrift flips," and vintage "style with me" videos continued to rack up millions of views, with one creator's oversized-blazer flip reportedly hitting 3.1 million views in a single week. Behind the entertainment is a hard number: secondhand clothing sales have surged, with reports citing a roughly 40% global increase, and a resale market growing several times faster than conventional retail. What began as bargain-hunting has matured into a genuine cultural shift — and a live experiment in building a circular economy from the bottom up.

The Social Innovation Angle

Fast fashion is one of the defining social and environmental failures of our era: enormous waste, exploited labor, and a throwaway logic that treats clothing as disposable. Traditional fixes have come from the top — regulation, corporate sustainability pledges, recycling schemes — and have largely failed to bend the curve. The viral thrift movement is a different kind of solution, and it carries the fingerprints of social innovation.

First, it changes the default. For years, buying used carried a whiff of necessity or shame. Creators inverted that meaning, making secondhand a marker of taste, resourcefulness, and individuality rather than poverty. Notably, consumer surveys now rank the top motivations as value, quality, and uniqueness — with sustainability lower down the list. That is strategically important: the movement succeeds precisely because it does not depend on altruism. It makes the sustainable choice the stylish, economical, and fun one. Social innovation that requires people to sacrifice rarely scales; innovation that aligns self-interest with the common good does.

Second, it redistributes economic agency. Resale platforms and thrift culture turn ordinary people into micro-entrepreneurs and curators. A teenager can source, restyle, and resell; a household can extract value from a closet instead of sending it to landfill. This is a decentralization of the fashion economy away from a handful of brands and toward a distributed network of individuals — precisely the kind of power shift that defines social innovation, not merely social improvement.

Third, it prototypes circularity at population scale. Every garment kept in use is a small act of climate mitigation, and the thrift movement is running that experiment across millions of wardrobes without waiting for a policy mandate. It demonstrates demand for durability, repairability, and reuse — signals that can, in turn, pressure manufacturers and inform regulation. Grassroots behavior becomes evidence for systemic change.

The risks are real and worth confronting. Resale can become "recommerce theatre," where brands use it to greenwash continued overproduction. Rising demand has pushed up thrift prices in some markets, risking the displacement of the low-income shoppers who always relied on secondhand out of necessity — a justice concern that the trend's affluent participants often ignore. And virality favors constant novelty, which can quietly reintroduce the very churn that thrift was supposed to slow. A movement about consuming less can be captured by an aesthetic of always buying more, just secondhand.

Actionable Takeaways

\- For policymakers: back the grassroots signal with structure — right-to-repair rules, textile-waste bans, and tax incentives for resale and repair over disposal. The public appetite is now demonstrated; meet it with infrastructure.  
\- For brands: move from resale-as-marketing to resale-as-business-model. Design for durability and take-back; the credible players will be those who reduce production, not those who add a resale tab.  
\- For community organizations: protect access. As thrift gentrifies, invest in clothing libraries, swap events, and repair cafés so reuse stays affordable for those who need it most, not just those who find it trendy.  
\- For consumers: keep the point in view — buy less, keep longer, repair first. Thrifting a haul you will discard next month is fast fashion in disguise.

The deeper lesson is that a durable circular economy may be built less by mandates than by millions of people deciding, one outfit at a time, that reuse is the more desirable choice.

引言

本週統治時尚圈的不是伸展台，而是一堆翻找。爆紅的二手開箱（thrift haul）、「舊衣改造（thrift flip）」與古著「陪我穿搭」影片，持續累積數百萬觀看，其中一位創作者的「寬版西裝外套改造」據報導單週衝上 310 萬觀看。娛樂背後是一組硬數據：二手服飾銷售暴增，有報導引用約 40% 的全球成長，而轉售市場的成長速度更是傳統零售的數倍。這場從「撿便宜」起家的風潮，如今已成熟為一場真正的文化轉向——也是一場由下而上打造循環經濟的現場實驗。

社會創新角度分析

快時尚是我們這個時代最具代表性的社會與環境失敗之一：驚人的浪費、被剝削的勞動、以及把衣服當作用完即丟的邏輯。傳統的解方多半來自上層——法規、企業永續承諾、回收計畫——卻幾乎未能扭轉曲線。爆紅的二手運動是另一種解方，而且帶著鮮明的社會創新指紋。

第一，它改變了「預設值」。多年來，買二手總帶著一絲「不得已」或羞恥的氣味。創作者把這層意涵徹底反轉，讓二手成為品味、機智與個人風格的標記，而非貧窮的象徵。值得注意的是，如今消費者調查將購買動機排序為：價值、品質、獨特性——「永續」反而排在後面。這在策略上極為重要：這場運動之所以成功，正因為它不依賴利他心。它讓永續選項同時成為時尚、划算又好玩的選項。需要人們「犧牲」的社會創新很難擴散；讓個人利益與公共利益一致的創新，才會成長。

第二，它重新分配了經濟能動性。轉售平台與二手文化，把一般人變成微型創業者與策展人。青少年可以尋貨、改造、再售；一個家庭可以從衣櫃裡榨出價值，而不是把衣服送進掩埋場。這是一種把時尚經濟從少數品牌手中「去中心化」、轉向由個人組成的分散網絡的過程——而這種權力的位移，正是「社會創新」有別於單純「社會改善」的關鍵所在。

第三，它在「人口尺度」上為循環經濟做原型測試。每一件被持續使用的衣服，都是一次微小的氣候減緩行動，而二手運動正在數百萬個衣櫃裡跑這場實驗，不必等待任何政策命令。它證明了市場對「耐用、可修、可重複使用」的需求存在——而這些訊號，反過來可以對製造商施壓、並為法規提供依據。草根行為，成為系統性改變的證據。

必須正面面對的風險同樣真實。轉售可能淪為「再商務劇場」，品牌用它來為持續的過度生產「漂綠」。需求上升已在部分市場推高二手價格，可能排擠那些一直以來因為「不得已」而依賴二手的低收入者——這是趨勢中富裕參與者常忽略的正義議題。而「爆紅」偏好源源不絕的新鮮感，這可能悄悄把二手原本想放慢的「汰換循環」又重新引進來。一場關於「少消費」的運動，很可能被一種「永遠買更多、只是買二手」的美學所綁架。

可行的啟示與行動建議

\- 給政策制定者：用制度承接草根訊號——推動維修權（right-to-repair）、紡織廢棄物禁令，以及「轉售與維修優於丟棄」的稅務誘因。大眾胃口已被證明存在，就用基礎設施去接住它。  
\- 給品牌：從「把轉售當行銷」走向「把轉售當商業模式」。為耐用與回收（take-back）而設計；有公信力的玩家，會是那些「減少生產」的，而不是多加一個轉售分頁的。  
\- 給社區組織：守護「取得管道」。當二手被「仕紳化」，投資衣物圖書館、以物易物活動與維修咖啡館，讓重複使用對最需要的人維持可負擔，而不只是對覺得它時髦的人。  
\- 給消費者：別忘了初衷——少買、久穿、先修再說。買一堆下個月就丟的二手戰利品，只是換了個外衣的快時尚。

更深的啟示是：一個耐久的循環經濟，或許不是靠命令建成，而是靠數百萬人一次一套穿搭地決定——重複使用，才是更令人嚮往的選擇。

\[EN\] The Overtourism Backlash and the "Coolcation": Redistributing the Right to Travel  
────────────────────────────

Introduction

This week travel headlines converged on a striking behavioral shift: a reported 43% of travelers globally now say they plan to avoid overcrowded destinations in 2026 — an 11-point jump on the year before. The story took concrete form in the "coolcation," with Copenhagen Airport moving over 3.45 million passengers in a single month as tourists trade the sweltering, saturated south of Europe for cooler, quieter north. Meanwhile destinations from the Canary Islands to Venice keep tightening rules as record crowds strain the places locals call home. Overtourism has stopped being an industry footnote and become a mainstream lifestyle question: how, and where, should we travel?

The Social Innovation Angle

Overtourism is a textbook "tragedy of the commons": a shared resource — a city, a coastline, a culture — degraded because individually rational trips add up to collective harm. Residents are priced out, infrastructure buckles, and the very thing tourists came for is eroded. The emerging response has the shape of social innovation, though it is still unevenly formed.

First, demand itself is being redesigned from the bottom up. The choice to avoid crowded hotspots is a distributed, voluntary correction — millions of individual decisions redirecting flows toward under-visited places and off-peak times. This spreads economic benefit to communities that rarely see tourist money and relieves pressure on saturated ones. When consumer behavior reorganizes to reduce a collective harm without being forced to, that is social innovation operating through culture rather than regulation.

Second, destinations are prototyping new governance of the commons. Visitor caps, timed entry, tourist taxes ring-fenced for local housing and services, and limits on cruise arrivals are experiments in managing a shared asset for residents first. The most innovative are not merely defensive; they redistribute value — channeling tourism revenue into the community that bears its costs, and giving locals a formal voice in how many visitors their home can hold. This is a governance innovation: treating a destination as a commons to be stewarded, not a product to be sold without limit.

Third, a "regenerative tourism" ethic is moving from niche to mainstream conversation — the idea that travel should leave a place better than it was found, funding conservation and local livelihoods rather than extracting from them. That reframes the traveler from consumer to temporary stakeholder, a subtle but powerful shift in the social contract of tourism.

But the risks and injustices are sharp. "Avoid the crowds" is, for now, a privilege of the informed and affluent; the coolcation can simply export the overtourism problem to the next fragile, unprepared town. Redirecting crowds without governance turns yesterday's hidden gem into tomorrow's overrun casualty. And climate is the uncomfortable backdrop: cooler-north travel is partly a flight from extreme heat, which the flights themselves worsen — a feedback loop the trend rarely admits. Social innovation here means more than dispersing tourists; it means redistributing the benefits and the say.

Actionable Takeaways

\- For destination governments: manage the commons deliberately. Pair visitor caps with taxes ring-fenced for housing and services, and give residents a real role in setting limits. Redistribute the revenue to those who bear the cost.  
\- For the travel industry: build products that spread benefit — promote shoulder seasons and lesser-known regions, cap group sizes, and partner with local enterprises so more of the spend stays local.  
\- For communities newly "discovered": get ahead of the wave. Set carrying-capacity rules before the crowds arrive, not after the damage is done.  
\- For travelers: go slower, stay longer, spend locally, and travel off-peak. Choose fewer, deeper trips over more, shallower ones — and weigh the climate cost of chasing cooler air.

The lasting insight is that the right to enjoy the world's shared places is being renegotiated in real time — and whether that ends in fairer, more sustainable travel depends on pairing millions of individual choices with the governance to make them add up to something just.

引言

本週旅遊新聞不約而同指向一個顯著的行為轉變：據報導，全球有 43% 的旅客表示，2026 年將刻意避開人潮擁擠的景點——比前一年一口氣跳升了 11 個百分點。這個故事以「涼假（coolcation）」的形式具體化：哥本哈根機場單月吞吐超過 345 萬人次，因為旅客把悶熱又飽和的南歐，換成了涼爽而清靜的北方。與此同時，從加那利群島到威尼斯，各地持續收緊規則，因為破紀錄的人潮正壓垮當地居民賴以為家的地方。過度觀光不再是產業的註腳，而成了主流的生活課題：我們該如何、又該去哪裡旅行？

社會創新角度分析

過度觀光是教科書級的「公地悲劇」：一項共享資源——一座城市、一片海岸、一種文化——因為每一趟「個別理性」的旅程加總起來，造成了集體傷害而遭到破壞。居民被高房價逼走，基礎設施不堪負荷，而旅客當初前來追尋的東西，正被侵蝕殆盡。新興的回應帶有社會創新的雛形，儘管它的成形仍不均勻。

第一，「需求」本身正被由下而上地重新設計。選擇避開擁擠熱點，是一種分散式、自願性的修正——數百萬個個人決定，把人流重新導向少有人造訪的地方與離峰時段。這把經濟利益擴散到那些鮮少見到觀光財的社區，同時紓解了飽和地區的壓力。當消費行為在「不被強制」的情況下自我重組、以減少集體傷害，這就是社會創新透過文化、而非法規，在運作。

第二，各目的地正在為「公地」試作新的治理模式。人數上限、分時入場、將稅收專款專用於在地住房與服務的觀光稅、以及限制郵輪靠港——都是「以居民為先」來管理共享資產的實驗。最具創新性的做法不只是防禦性的；它們重新分配了價值——把觀光收入導入承擔其成本的社區，並讓在地人對「自己的家能容納多少訪客」擁有正式的發言權。這是一種治理創新：把目的地視為需要被「守護的公地」，而非可以無上限販售的「商品」。

第三，「再生式旅遊（regenerative tourism）」的倫理，正從小眾走向主流對話——其核心理念是：旅行應該讓一個地方變得比造訪前更好，為保育與在地生計注入資金，而不是從中榨取。這把旅人從「消費者」重新定義為「暫時的利害關係人」，是旅遊社會契約中一個微妙卻有力的轉變。

但風險與不公同樣尖銳。「避開人潮」目前仍是「有資訊、有錢」者的特權；涼假很可能只是把過度觀光的問題，輸出到下一個脆弱又毫無準備的小鎮。缺乏治理的人流重導，會把昨日的隱藏珍寶，變成明日被淹沒的犧牲者。而氣候是那個令人不安的背景：往涼爽北方的旅行，部分正是為了逃離極端高溫，而這些航班本身又加劇了高溫——這是趨勢很少承認的回饋迴圈。此處的社會創新，意義遠不只是「疏散旅客」，而是「重新分配利益與話語權」。

可行的啟示與行動建議

\- 給目的地政府：刻意地治理公地。把人數上限與「專款專用於住房與服務」的稅收綁在一起，並讓居民在設定上限時扮演實質角色。把收入重新分配給承擔成本的人。  
\- 給旅遊產業：打造「擴散利益」的產品——推廣淡季與較不知名的區域、限制團體人數、與在地企業合作，讓更多消費留在當地。  
\- 給剛被「發現」的社區：搶在浪潮之前。在人潮抵達「之前」就設定承載量規則，而不是等傷害造成之後。  
\- 給旅客：走慢一點、待久一點、在地消費、避開尖峰。選擇「更少而更深」的旅程，勝過「更多而更淺」的——並且，衡量一下「追逐涼爽空氣」的氣候代價。

真正的啟示是：享用世界共享之地的權利，正在此刻被重新協商——而這場協商最終會不會走向更公平、更永續的旅行，取決於我們能否讓數百萬個個人選擇，搭配上足以讓它們「加總成正義」的治理。
