**來源連結 / Source：** Convosight, “Viral Moments of November 2026” — [https\://www\.convosight.com/tools/moments/trends/2026-11](https://www.convosight.com/tools/moments/trends/2026-11)

### **English Draft**

**When the Oven Becomes a Workshop: Europe’s Christmas Baking Surge and the Social Economy Rising With It**  
*Introduction.* This week, one of the largest attention events on European social platforms was not a scandal or a new gadget. It was Christmas baking. Convosight’s viral tracker recorded about 33 million views on baking content across the region, enough to place home ovens among the week’s biggest viral moments, next to a hit Asian variety collaboration and India’s cosmetics clips. On the surface this is seasonal comfort content: phone footage of gingerbread houses, stollen, and cinnamon stars. Look closer and a different pattern appears. Millions of people are not only watching baking; they are producing it, selling it, teaching it, and using it to hold small communities together through a cold and uncertain winter. That is where a holiday hobby turns into material for social innovation.  
*A social innovation reading.* The first thing worth naming is that a home kitchen is a unit of production. Behind the pretty footage sits a large informal economy of people who bake for income, usually women, caregivers, recent migrants, students, and others the formal labour market serves poorly. A phone, a domestic oven, and a messaging app are now enough to reach paying customers without a shopfront or startup capital. The barrier to entry is close to zero, which is exactly what makes it an inclusion tool. The weakness is that the rules have not caught up. Across Europe, cottage-food regulation is a patchwork: some regions license home bakers with a short hygiene course, others treat any home sale as illegal food business, which pushes people into precarity and leaves customers unprotected. The social innovation opportunity here is regulatory design rather than a new app, light-touch home-kitchen licensing, pooled liability insurance sold in small monthly amounts, and shared commercial kitchens that let a baker scale up on the days a large order comes in.  
The second reading is about wellbeing. Repetitive, tactile, creative work such as kneading dough and decorating has a measurable calming effect, and health systems have started to use it on purpose. The United Kingdom’s social-prescribing model, where a doctor can refer a lonely or anxious patient to a community activity instead of, or alongside, medication, already includes community baking groups. Read against the rest of this week’s news, a run of technology outages and a viral blackout hoax, the baking surge looks partly like a collective coping behaviour: when screens feel unstable, people reach for something they can make with their hands and share at a table. Social innovators can build on that rather than leave it to chance, by funding community kitchens attached to libraries and care homes, pairing isolated older residents with younger neighbours who film and sell what they bake together, and treating the kitchen as infrastructure for mental health, not only for food.  
The third reading is circularity and solidarity. Festive baking produces surplus, and surplus is where a simple hobby can become a redistribution system. “Pay it forward” loaves left at community fridges, bakers who donate a fixed share of each batch, and recipes built around ingredients that would otherwise be thrown away all turn a seasonal spike in production into food security for people who need it. The most durable version of this is ownership. Instead of home bakers depending on marketplaces that take a large cut and can change the rules overnight, a baker cooperative or a platform cooperative lets the people doing the work share the margin and the decisions. That shift, from gig worker to member owner, is the difference between a trend that enriches a platform and one that builds lasting community wealth.  
*Insights and practical actions.* For local governments: pilot a simple home-kitchen licence and publish it in plain language before the next festive season, so informal bakers can trade legally rather than in fear. For social enterprises and foundations: fund shared commercial kitchens and a small-premium insurance product aimed at home food producers, the two things that most often block a hobby from becoming a livelihood. For health and care services: add community baking to social-prescribing menus and measure loneliness, not only output. For platforms and civic technologists: support baker-owned cooperatives and open directories instead of extractive marketplaces. For individuals who caught the trend: route surplus through a community fridge, teach one neighbour, and buy from a home baker before a supermarket. The viral clip lasts a day. The kitchen, if we treat it as shared infrastructure, can carry a neighbourhood through the winter and well past it.

**當烤箱變成工作坊：歐洲耶誕烘焙熱潮，與它背後正在長出的社會經濟**  
*引言。*這一週，歐洲社群平台上聲量最高的事件之一，既不是醜聞也不是新科技產品，而是「耶誕烘焙」。社群數據機構 Convosight 的爆紅追蹤顯示，烘焙內容一週累積約 3,300 萬次觀看，讓家庭烤箱擠進本週全球十大爆紅時刻，和亞洲人氣綜藝聯名、印度美妝短片並列。表面上，這只是入冬的療癒內容：薑餅屋、史多倫麵包、肉桂星餅的手機隨拍。但再看一層會發現另一種樣貌：數以百萬計的人不只是在「看」烘焙，他們同時在生產、販售、教學，並用這件事把一個個小社群，撐過寒冷又不安的冬天。熱門話題到了這裡，就成了值得社會創新工作者認真看待的素材。  
*從社會創新角度分析。*第一個值得指認的事實是：家庭廚房本身就是一個生產單位。漂亮影片的背後，是一個龐大的非正式經濟，裡面多半是婦女、照顧者、新移民、學生，以及其他被正規勞動市場邊緣化的人。如今只要一支手機、一台家用烤箱、一個通訊軟體，就能在沒有店面、沒有創業資金的情況下接觸到付費客人。進入門檻幾乎是零，這正是它可以成為社會「共融工具」的原因。問題在於制度沒有跟上：歐洲各地對「家庭食品」的規範零散不一，有些地方讓家庭烘焙者上個衛生課程就能合法販售，有些則把任何居家販售都當成非法食品業，結果一邊把人推向更不穩定的處境，一邊讓消費者缺乏保障。這裡的社會創新機會，與其說是再做一個 App，不如說是「制度設計」：輕量化的家庭廚房登記制、以小額月費分攤的責任保險，以及讓接到大單的烘焙者臨時擴產的共享商用廚房。  
第二個切點是身心健康。像揉麵團、裝飾餅乾這類重複、觸覺性、帶創作感的勞動，對情緒有可量測的安定作用，醫療體系也開始刻意運用它。英國推動的「社會處方」制度，允許醫師把孤單或焦慮的病人轉介到社區活動，而非只開藥，當中早已包含社區烘焙團體。對照本週其他新聞，接連的科技服務中斷，加上瘋傳的「全球大停電」假消息，這波烘焙熱其實帶著集體調適的味道：當螢幕讓人感到不穩，人們便回頭尋找一件能親手做、能端上桌分享的事。社會創新者可以順勢而為，而不是任其隨機發生，例如在圖書館、長照機構旁設置社區廚房，讓獨居長者與年輕鄰居配對，一起烘焙、一起拍、一起賣，把廚房當成心理健康的基礎設施，而不只是做菜的地方。  
第三個切點是循環與互助。節慶烘焙必然產生剩餘，而「剩餘」正是一個單純嗜好可以升級為再分配系統的地方。擺在社區冰箱裡的「待用麵包」、每批固定捐出一定比例的烘焙者、以及刻意使用即將被丟棄食材所設計的食譜，都能把季節性的產量高峰，轉化為有需要者的糧食安全。而最能長久的版本，關鍵在「所有權」。與其讓家庭烘焙者依附在抽成高、又可能一夜改規則的平台上，不如組成烘焙合作社或平台合作社，讓真正動手做的人分享利潤，也分享決策。從「零工」變成「會員兼股東」，正是「讓平台賺飽」與「讓社區累積長久財富」兩種結果之間的分水嶺。  
*可行的啟示與行動建議。*給地方政府：在下一個節慶旺季到來前，試辦一套簡單的家庭廚房登記制，並用白話公布，讓非正式烘焙者能合法經營，而不是提心吊膽地做。給社會企業與基金會：投入共享商用廚房，以及針對家庭食品生產者設計的低保費保險，這兩項往往是嗜好能否變成生計的最大卡關點。給醫療與照顧服務：把社區烘焙納入社會處方菜單，並以「孤獨感是否下降」而非只用「產量」來衡量成效。給平台與公民科技工作者：支持烘焙者自己擁有的合作社與公開名錄，取代高抽成的掠奪式市集。給被這波熱潮打中的個人：把多做的份量送進社區冰箱、教會一位鄰居、優先向家庭烘焙者而非超市購買。一支爆紅影片只熱一天；但只要我們把廚房當成共享的基礎設施，它能帶著一個街區走過整個冬天，也走得更遠。

## **熱點二（住）：老宅咖啡廳活化與快閃店　The Old House Reopens**

**來源連結 / Source：** 民眾新聞網，〈新北板橋老宅咖啡廳變身　庸俗救星快閃店免費逛到 11/22〉 — [https\://mypeoplevol.com/2026/life/121083](https://mypeoplevol.com/2026/life/121083)

### **English Draft**

**The Old House Reopens: What a Pop-Up in a Converted Café Tells Us About Reusing Cities**  
*Introduction.* Among this week’s lifestyle stories in Taiwan, a small one travelled far: a pop-up shop by the local brand “Common Saviour” (庸俗救星) opened inside Xujiu Coffee (旭舊咖啡), a café built into an old house in Banqiao, New Taipei. It is free to visit and runs only until 22 November. The format is modest, an aged residence turned into a café, hosting a short-run shop for a couple of weeks, yet it drew crowds and online attention out of proportion to its size. That gap between a small footprint and a large response is the interesting part. It points to an appetite, especially among younger city dwellers, for places that feel specific, reused, and alive, rather than new, generic, and permanent. For anyone working on housing, heritage, and local regeneration, this is a live case study in adaptive reuse.  
*A social innovation reading.* The core idea on display is adaptive reuse: taking a building that has outlived its first purpose and giving it a new one instead of demolishing it. In dense Asian cities, old low-rise houses are usually seen as obstacles to redevelopment, worth more knocked down than kept. Converting one into a café, and then letting a pop-up borrow it for two weeks, quietly argues the opposite. It says the structure already standing has value: embodied carbon that was spent decades ago and does not need spending again, a human scale that new towers rarely offer, and a memory of the neighbourhood that residents recognise. Demolition throws all of that away. The environmental case alone is strong, because the greenest building is often the one that already exists, and reuse avoids both the emissions of demolition and the emissions of new construction.  
The second reading is about risk and the pop-up as a method. A permanent shop is a heavy bet: a long lease, a fit-out, staff, and months before anyone knows whether the location works. A two-week pop-up inside an existing café strips that risk down to almost nothing. It is, in effect, a prototype for a place, a way to test whether people will come, what they will pay for, and what the space wants to become, before committing capital. This is where temporary use becomes a genuine innovation tool rather than a marketing gimmick. Cities that want their high streets and old quarters to come back to life can treat vacancy as a testing ground: a register of empty buildings, short and cheap licences, and a light legal template that lets an owner lend a space to a maker for a month without fear. Each pop-up that succeeds is evidence for a longer tenancy; each that fails costs almost nothing. The whole district learns faster.  
The third reading is who captures the value. Regeneration has a familiar failure: a neighbourhood becomes interesting, rents rise, and the independent makers and long-time residents who made it interesting are pushed out, replaced by chains that can pay. The result looks lively but is hollow, the same brands found everywhere. The more durable model keeps ownership and benefit local. That can mean a community land trust or a cooperative that holds the old building so its rent cannot be bid up without limit, revenue-sharing between the host café and the visiting pop-up so both gain, and a deliberate pipeline that rotates local makers through the space rather than auctioning it to the highest national bidder. The goal is not a single charming afternoon but a street that still belongs to the people who live on it in five years.  
*Insights and practical actions.* For city governments: build a public register of vacant and underused buildings, and create a fast, low-cost temporary-use permit so a pop-up can open legally in days rather than months. For building owners: a short lend to a maker is cheaper than an empty property and is the lowest-risk way to find out what a space can hold. For cultural and regeneration funders: back community land trusts and cooperatives that keep old buildings affordable, so success does not automatically price out the people who created it. For local makers and café owners: share space and revenue rather than compete for footfall, and document what each pop-up teaches so the next one starts further ahead. For residents: show up, because in reuse economics attention is the signal that keeps a building standing. A pop-up that lasts to 22 November is small. The method behind it, reuse before rebuild, test before you commit, and keep the value local, can reshape how a whole city grows.

**老宅重新開門：一間改造咖啡廳裡的快閃店，教我們如何「再利用」一座城市**  
*引言。*本週台灣的生活新聞裡，有一則規模不大、卻傳得很遠：在地品牌「庸俗救星」在新北板橋一間由老宅改造的「旭舊咖啡」裡開設快閃店，免費入場，而且只到 11 月 22 日。形式很樸素，一棟老房子變成咖啡廳，再借出兩週給一家短期小店，卻吸引到與它體量不成比例的人潮與網路討論。這種「小空間、大迴響」的落差，正是耐人尋味之處。它反映出一種需求，尤其在年輕都市人之間：人們渴望那種有個性、被再利用、仍然活著的場所，而不是全新、制式、永久的空間。對任何在做居住、文資保存與地方再生的人來說，這就是一則正在發生的「老屋活化」案例。  
*從社會創新角度分析。*這則熱點呈現的核心概念是「適應性再利用」（adaptive reuse）：把一棟已經不再適合原本用途的建築，不拆掉，而是賦予新用途。在高密度的亞洲城市裡，老舊低矮的房子常被視為都更的障礙，彷彿拆掉比保留更值錢。把它改造成咖啡廳，再讓快閃店借用兩週，等於安靜地提出相反的主張：這棟已經站在那裡的建築本身就有價值，它的「蘊含碳」早在幾十年前就花掉了，不必再花一次；它擁有新大樓很少能給的人性尺度，也承載著居民認得的街區記憶。拆除，等於把這些一次丟光。單就環境而論，理由就已經很強，因為最環保的建築往往就是「已經存在的那一棟」，再利用可以同時避免拆除與新建兩段的碳排。  
第二個切點，是關於風險，以及「快閃」作為一種方法。開一家永久店是很重的賭注：長期租約、裝修、人事，還要等上好幾個月，才知道這個地點行不行。相對地，在既有咖啡廳裡開兩週的快閃店，幾乎把風險降到趨近於零。它實際上是一種「地方的原型測試」，在投入大筆資金之前，先驗證人們會不會來、願意為什麼付錢、這個空間適合長成什麼模樣。這正是臨時性使用（temporary use）從行銷噱頭，升級為真正創新工具的地方。想讓老街區與舊城區重新活絡的城市，可以把「閒置」當成試驗場：建立空屋清單、提供短期又便宜的許可、以及一份輕量的法律範本，讓屋主能安心把空間借給創作者一個月。每一次成功的快閃，都是延長租約的證據；每一次失敗，成本幾乎為零。整個街區因此學得更快。  
第三個切點，是「價值由誰獲得」。地方再生有一個常見的失敗劇本：一個街區變得有趣，租金隨之上漲，當初讓它變有趣的獨立創作者與老住戶被擠出去，換成付得起租金的連鎖店進駐。結果看起來熱鬧，實則空洞，到處都是同樣的品牌。比較能長久的模式，是把所有權與利益留在地方。這可能意味著由社區土地信託（community land trust）或合作社持有這棟老建築，讓租金不致被無上限地喊高；也可能是主場咖啡廳與進駐快閃店之間的拆帳分潤，讓雙方都受益；或是刻意設計一條輪替管道，讓在地創作者輪流使用這個空間，而不是把它拍賣給出價最高的全國品牌。目標不是一個迷人的下午，而是五年後，這條街仍然屬於住在上面的人。  
*可行的啟示與行動建議。*給城市政府：建立一份公開的空屋與低度使用建築清單，並設計一套快速、低成本的臨時使用許可，讓快閃店能在幾天內、而非幾個月後合法開張。給屋主：把空間短期借給創作者，比讓房子空著更划算，也是了解一個空間能承載什麼的最低風險方式。給文化與再生領域的出資者：支持能讓老建築維持可負擔租金的社區土地信託與合作社，別讓「成功」自動把當初創造價值的人趕走。給在地創作者與咖啡廳主：與其搶人流，不如共享空間與分潤，並把每次快閃學到的經驗記錄下來，讓下一棒站在更前面的起點。給居民：請真的出現，因為在再利用的經濟學裡，「關注」就是讓一棟建築得以繼續站著的訊號。一家開到 11 月 22 日的快閃店很小，但它背後的方法，先再利用再重建、先測試再投入、把價值留在地方，足以重塑一整座城市的生長方式。

## **熱點三（行）：年末旅展與超低價機票　The 999 Fare**

**來源連結 / Source：** 鏡週刊，〈搶票倒數！2026 ITF 旅展航空優惠懶人包　虎航 999 元起、華航 76 折、星宇滿 2 萬抽商務艙〉 — [https\://www\.mirrormedia.mg/story/20261108tour002](https://www.mirrormedia.mg/story/20261108tour002)

### **English Draft**

**The 999 Fare: Cheap Flights, Crowded Places, and the Design of Fairer Travel**  
*Introduction.* As Taiwan’s year-end travel-fair season opened this week, one offer did the loudest work: single fares from NT\$999 before tax across Tigerair Taiwan’s whole network, with Taipei to Okinawa and Taipei to Ishigaki among the cheapest routes, alongside discounts from China Airlines, EVA Air, and Starlux. A thousand New Taiwan dollars is roughly the price of two cinema tickets, and for that a person can cross a sea. The response was immediate, with tickets selling fast before the window closed. Ultra-cheap flights are easy to cheer and easy to condemn, and both reactions miss the more useful question. Cheap mobility is one of the strongest forces for opening up the world and one of the strongest drivers of its environmental strain at the same time. The social innovation task is not to pick a side but to design travel that is both widely accessible and less harmful.  
*A social innovation reading.* Start with what cheap flying gets right, because dismissing it as frivolous misreads who it serves. Low fares turn travel from a privilege of the wealthy into something a student, a factory worker, or a family on a tight budget can occasionally afford. People visit relatives working abroad, see a different society with their own eyes, and gain the confidence and perspective that moving through the world provides. Treating budget travel as purely wasteful quietly assumes that only the well-off deserve to see the world. A social innovation stance holds on to that gain in access while refusing to ignore the cost that comes with it.  
The cost has two parts, and each has a design answer. The first is carbon. Aviation is hard to decarbonise, and a NT\$999 fare makes a flight almost an impulse, so volume rises. The honest response is not to lecture individuals but to change the system around the ticket, by scaling sustainable aviation fuel through advance purchase commitments that give producers the certainty to build supply, by pricing carbon into the fare so the cost is visible rather than hidden, and by investing in fast rail for the short routes where a train can replace a plane outright. The aim is to decouple the freedom to move from the damage of moving, so that access does not have to mean harm. The second cost is overtourism. When a handful of destinations go viral and a cheap seat removes the last barrier, the same few places are overwhelmed, their housing is converted to short lets, their residents are priced out, and the visitor experience degrades for everyone. The design answer is redistribution: steer cheap routes and incentives toward second cities and rural regions that want visitors and have capacity, rather than piling more arrivals onto the handful already past their limit.  
The third reading turns tourism itself into a tool. A visit can extract value from a place or build it, and the difference is a matter of design. Community-based tourism, where lodging, food, and guiding are owned and run by residents, keeps the money in local hands instead of leaking it to outside operators. The same cheap seat that fills an overcrowded hotspot could instead carry a traveller to a village running its own homestay network, a cooperative-led food trail, or a conservation project that needs both income and witnesses. Low fares are a powerful piece of infrastructure. Whether that infrastructure concentrates harm in a few saturated places or spreads benefit across many that want it depends on what public bodies, airlines, and platforms choose to steer it toward.  
*Insights and practical actions.* For airlines: pair headline fares with advance purchase of sustainable aviation fuel and clear carbon pricing in the ticket, so growth and decarbonisation move together rather than against each other. For tourism boards: direct subsidies and promotion toward regions with spare capacity, and set limits where places are already saturated, rather than marketing the same postcard destinations. For travel platforms: make community-owned stays and local operators easy to find and book, so a budget traveller can choose the option that keeps money local. For city governments at the receiving end: protect housing from conversion to short lets before a destination tips into overtourism, not after. For travellers: use the cheap seat to go somewhere that wants you, book local and stay longer rather than hopping between crowded hotspots, and treat the carbon as a real cost to account for, not to ignore. A NT\$999 fare is not the problem or the solution. It is infrastructure, and infrastructure follows the incentives we build around it.

**999 元機票：便宜的航班、擁擠的景點，與「更公平的旅行」該怎麼設計**  
*引言。*隨著台灣年末旅展季在本週開跑，最響亮的一檔優惠非它莫屬：台灣虎航全航線單程未稅 999 元起，台北飛沖繩、台北飛石垣島名列最低價航線，華航、長榮、星宇也同步推出折扣。一千元新台幣，大約是兩張電影票的價錢，有了它，一個人就能飛越一片海。市場反應立刻熱烈，開賣後機票在期限內迅速被搶光。超低價機票很容易被喝采，也很容易被譴責，但這兩種反應都錯過了更有用的問題。便宜的移動力，同時是「打開世界」最強的力量之一，也是「加重環境負擔」最強的推手之一。社會創新要做的，不是選邊站，而是設計出一種既能普及、又更少傷害的旅行。  
*從社會創新角度分析。*先談便宜航班做對了什麼，因為把它斥為輕浮，等於誤判了它服務的對象。低票價讓旅行從有錢人的特權，變成學生、工廠勞工、預算緊繃的家庭偶爾也負擔得起的事。人們得以探望在外地工作的親人、親眼看見另一個社會的樣貌，並獲得在世界上移動所帶來的視野與自信。把廉價旅行一概視為浪費，背後其實暗藏一個假設：只有家境優渥的人才配看世界。社會創新的立場，是一方面緊緊守住這份「可及性」的益處，一方面也拒絕對隨之而來的代價視而不見。  
這份代價有兩個部分，而每一部分都有對應的「設計解法」。第一是碳排。航空業本就難以減碳，而一張 999 元的機票幾乎讓搭機變成一時衝動，於是運量上升。誠實的回應不是去教訓個人，而是改變票券周邊的整個系統：透過「預先採購承諾」擴大永續航空燃料的規模，讓生產者有足夠確定性去建置供應；把碳成本計入票價，讓代價被看見而非被隱藏；並在火車能完全取代飛機的短程路線上，投資高速鐵路。目標是把「移動的自由」與「移動的傷害」脫鉤，讓「可及」不必然等於「有害」。第二個代價是過度旅遊。當少數景點爆紅，而廉價座位又拆掉了最後一道門檻，同樣那幾個地方就被塞爆：住宅被改成短租、居民被房價擠出、旅遊品質對所有人都變差。設計上的解方是「再分配」：把廉價航線與獎勵，導向那些歡迎旅客、也還有承載餘裕的二線城市與鄉村地區，而不是把更多人潮，堆到那少數早已超載的地方。  
第三個切點，是把「旅遊」本身變成一種工具。一次造訪，可以從一個地方抽取價值，也可以為它累積價值，兩者的差別在於「設計」。社區型旅遊（community-based tourism），讓住宿、餐飲與導覽都由在地居民擁有並經營，使收入留在當地人手中，而不是外流給外來業者。同樣一張廉價座位，與其把人送進人滿為患的熱點，不如載著旅人前往一個自營民宿網絡的村落、一條合作社經營的美食路線，或一個同時需要收入與見證者的保育計畫。低票價是一項強大的基礎設施；這項基礎設施究竟是把傷害集中在少數飽和之地，還是把益處擴散到許多渴望旅客的地方，取決於公部門、航空公司與平台選擇把它導向何方。  
*可行的啟示與行動建議。*給航空公司：在推出話題票價的同時，搭配永續航空燃料的預先採購，並在票價中清楚計入碳成本，讓「成長」與「減碳」同向而行，而非互相抵銷。給觀光主管機關：把補助與行銷資源導向仍有餘裕的地區，並在已飽和之處設下人數上限，而不是一再推銷那幾個明信片景點。給旅遊平台：讓社區自營的住宿與在地業者更容易被找到、被預訂，使預算型旅客能選擇「把錢留在當地」的選項。給承接人潮的地方政府：在景點倒向過度旅遊「之前」就保護住宅、限制改作短租，而不是等事後才補救。給旅人：用這張廉價機票去一個「歡迎你」的地方，優先預訂在地、延長停留，而非在擁擠熱點之間跳點，並把碳排當成一筆要認真計算的真實成本，而非一筆可以忽略的帳。一張 999 元的機票，既不是問題，也不是解答。它是一項基礎設施，而基礎設施，會順著我們為它建立的誘因走。
