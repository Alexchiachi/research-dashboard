### **English Version**

**Title: The Corner Store as Destination — What Convenience-Store Tourism Teaches Us About Everyday Social Innovation**  
**Introduction.** In 2026, one of the most surprising travel obsessions has nothing to do with monuments, beaches, or Michelin stars. Instead, tourists from Europe, North America, and beyond are booking flights to Tokyo, Seoul, Bangkok, and Singapore in part to shop at convenience stores. Google searches for "7/11 Japan" reportedly exploded by around 5,000%, and social feeds overflow with videos of onigiri unwrapping tutorials, strawberry-cream sandwiches, matcha lattes, and hot-food counters. What looks at first like a quirky internet fad is, on closer inspection, a revealing case study in how ordinary infrastructure can become a site of meaning, connection, and value creation.  
**A social innovation lens.** Social innovation is often defined as new solutions that simultaneously meet social needs and create new social relationships or collaborations. The Asian konbini — a portmanteau of "convenience" — is a quietly radical example. Over decades, chains like 7-Eleven Japan, Lawson, FamilyMart, and Korea's GS25 evolved from mere retail outlets into multi-functional civic hubs: you can pay utility bills, ship luggage, withdraw cash, buy concert tickets, print documents, and eat a fresh, affordable meal, all at 3 a.m. This is "social infrastructure" in the sociologist Eric Klinenberg's sense — physical places that shape the way people interact. The viral tourism moment simply made visible a public value that locals had long taken for granted.  
Three features make the konbini a social-innovation exemplar. First, **radical accessibility**: dense networks, 24-hour operation, and low price points mean the store meets basic needs across income levels and schedules, functioning as a de facto safety net for shift workers, the elderly living alone, and travelers. Second, **iterative, data-driven product design**: konbini chains famously rotate thousands of SKUs and refresh menus weekly based on point-of-sale data, effectively running a continuous, hyper-local innovation lab that responds to changing tastes and needs. Third, **dignity in the everyday**: an affordable, high-quality meal available anytime is not a luxury but a form of everyday equity — it democratizes convenience and care.  
The tourism surge also surfaces tensions worth naming. Over-tourism can strain neighborhood stores that were designed for locals; the aestheticization of konbini life can flatten the labor of low-paid staff and franchisees into "content"; and the export of the model risks copying its surface (cute packaging, viral snacks) while ignoring its substance (accessibility, reliability, integration into public life). Social innovation is not the snack — it is the system.  
**Insights and actionable recommendations.** For cities and civic designers: treat mundane retail as social infrastructure. Zoning, licensing, and public-service partnerships can encourage stores that offer bill payment, parcel pickup, community notice boards, or accessible restrooms — turning private convenience into public good. Struggling Western high streets and "food desert" neighborhoods could pilot konbini-style hybrid outlets combining groceries, fresh ready-meals, and light public services.  
For retailers and social enterprises: the lesson is designing for *everyone, all the time*. Adopt the konbini discipline of rapid, local iteration, but pair it with fair labor standards so the humans behind the counter share in the value their stores create. Consider "second-shelf" models that route surplus fresh food to community fridges rather than the bin.  
For travelers and creators: practice regenerative curiosity. Enjoy the konbini, but spend consciously, respect that these are working neighborhood services, and use your platform to spotlight the workers, suppliers, and civic functions — not just the strawberry sandwich. For policymakers in tourism, channel konbini interest into "everyday-life tourism" that spreads visitors beyond overcrowded landmarks and supports local economies.  
The deepest takeaway is a reframing: innovation need not be shiny or scarce. The convenience store — humble, ubiquitous, open all night — reminds us that some of the most powerful social technologies are the ones we walk past every day. Recognizing, protecting, and thoughtfully adapting them may be a more sustainable path to inclusive cities than chasing the next disruptive app.

### **中文版**

**標題：把「巷口超商」當成目的地——便利商店觀光帶給我們的日常社會創新啟示**  
**引言。**2026 年最令人意外的旅遊風潮之一，與名勝、海灘或米其林餐廳無關，而是──便利商店。來自歐洲、北美與世界各地的旅客，把日本東京、韓國首爾、泰國曼谷與新加坡的超商，列入行程清單。據報「7/11 Japan」的 Google 搜尋量暴增約 5,000%，社群上滿是拆御飯糰、草莓鮮奶油三明治、抹茶拿鐵與熱食櫃的影片。乍看之下這只是網路上的一時奇趣，細看卻是一個絕佳案例：它揭示了「平凡的基礎設施」如何成為意義、連結與價值創造的場域。  
**從社會創新的角度看。**社會創新常被定義為：能同時滿足社會需求、並創造新社會關係或協作的新方案。亞洲的「konbini」（便利商店的外來語簡稱）正是一個安靜而激進的範例。數十年來，日本 7-Eleven、Lawson、全家，以及韓國 GS25 等連鎖，從單純的零售據點，演化為多功能的公民樞紐：繳水電費、寄送行李、提領現金、買演唱會門票、列印文件、吃一頓新鮮又便宜的餐點，凌晨三點也辦得到。這正是社會學者克林南柏（Eric Klinenberg）所說的「社會性基礎設施」──形塑人們互動方式的實體場所。這波觀光熱潮，只是讓在地人早已習以為常的公共價值「被看見」了。  
便利商店之所以是社會創新的典範，有三項特徵。其一，**極致的可及性**：綿密的店網、24 小時營業與親民價格，使超商能跨越收入與作息差異滿足基本需求，實質上成為輪班工作者、獨居長者與旅人的安全網。其二，**反覆迭代、以數據驅動的商品設計**：超商以每週更新菜單、輪替數千項品項聞名，等於在經營一座持續運轉、極度在地化的創新實驗室，即時回應口味與需求的變化。其三，**日常中的尊嚴**：隨時可得、品質良好又負擔得起的一餐，並非奢侈，而是一種「日常的平權」──它讓便利與照顧變得人人可及。  
這波觀光熱也浮現值得指出的張力。過度觀光可能壓垮原本為在地服務而設計的店；把超商生活美學化，可能將低薪店員與加盟主的勞動，簡化成「內容素材」；而模式輸出時，也有只複製表象（可愛包裝、爆紅零食）卻忽略本質（可及性、可靠度、與公共生活的整合）的風險。社會創新不在於那份零食，而在於背後的系統。  
**啟示與可行的行動建議。**對城市與公共設計者而言：把不起眼的零售視為社會基礎設施。透過土地使用分區、執照與公共服務合作，鼓勵門市提供代收帳單、包裹寄取、社區公告欄或無障礙廁所，把私人的便利轉化為公共的好處。歐美陷入衰退的商店街，以及「食物沙漠」社區，都可以試辦超商式的複合據點，結合生鮮、即食餐點與輕量公共服務。  
對零售業者與社會企業而言：關鍵是為「所有人、所有時刻」而設計。學習超商快速、在地的迭代紀律，同時搭配公平的勞動標準，讓櫃檯後的人也能分享門市創造的價值。也可考慮「續食」模式，把新鮮的剩食導向社區冰箱，而非垃圾桶。  
對旅人與創作者而言：實踐「再生式的好奇心」。享受超商，但有意識地消費，尊重這些是服務在地的日常設施，並運用影響力照亮背後的員工、供應商與公共功能，而不只是那份草莓三明治。對觀光決策者而言，可把對超商的興趣，導向「日常生活觀光」，讓旅客分散到過度擁擠的地標之外，支持在地經濟。  
最深層的啟示是一次觀念的翻轉：創新不必然閃亮或稀有。那間謙卑、無所不在、徹夜不打烊的便利商店提醒我們──最強大的社會技術，往往是我們每天路過卻視而不見的那些。看見它、保護它、並用心加以調適，或許比追逐下一款顛覆式 App，更能通往包容而永續的城市。

### **English Version**

**Title: More Than a Menu Trend — How "Swicy" Reveals a Recipe for Inclusive Food Innovation**  
**Introduction.** "Swicy" — the mashup of sweet and spicy — has been simmering for years, but 2026 is the year it fully went mainstream. Hot honey drizzled on fried chicken and pizza was only the beginning; the trend has expanded into fruity, smoky, tangy, and creamy variations drawn from cuisines around the world. Surveys report that three of four consumers now say most foods taste better with some heat, and more than half of U.S. diners want to try unusual flavor pairings. Beneath the delicious surface lies a story about cultural exchange, small-producer economics, and how a shared taste can either open doors or quietly appropriate them.  
**A social innovation lens.** At first glance a flavor is not a social innovation. But food trends are powerful vectors of culture, livelihoods, and values, and swicy is instructive precisely because its DNA is cross-cultural. Sweet-heat combinations are ancient and global: Korean gochujang and honey, Mexican chamoy and tajín on fruit, Thai nam jim, Sichuanese sweet-and-spicy, West African suya, Caribbean jerk. The Western "swicy" boom is, in effect, a mainstream market catching up to flavor systems that diaspora and Indigenous communities have refined for generations. That creates both an opportunity and a responsibility.  
Consider the innovation dynamics. First, swicy is a **low-barrier platform for small producers**: hot-honey and chili-crisp makers, many of them family or immigrant-run micro-brands, have used the trend to scale from farmers' markets to national shelves, sometimes via partnerships with larger brands. A flavor movement can thus become an on-ramp to entrepreneurship. Second, swicy demonstrates **the power of "gateway" heat**: because the sweetness balances the burn, it broadens the tent rather than narrowing it, inviting cautious eaters into a wider world of spice and, by extension, of unfamiliar cuisines. Food can be a bridge that lowers cultural defensiveness. Third, chili and honey are **agriculturally significant**: rising demand can support pepper farmers and, crucially, pollinator-dependent beekeeping — a reminder that what trends on our plates ripples back to ecosystems and rural economies.  
Yet the same trend can go wrong. When large firms strip a flavor from its origin story, rename it, and capture the margins, the communities who created it are erased and under-compensated — the familiar pattern of culinary appropriation. Rapid demand can also incentivize unsustainable sourcing, exploitative labor in agriculture, and homogenized, additive-laden products that mimic "swicy" without nourishing anyone. The social value is not in the buzzword; it is in who benefits and who is honored.  
**Insights and actionable recommendations.** For chefs, brands, and retailers: practice *attribution and equity*. Name the cuisines and communities a dish draws from, source signature ingredients through fair-trade or direct-trade relationships with the farmers and makers who originated them, and co-create products with, not merely inspired by, those communities. Reserve shelf space and marketing for small and diaspora-owned producers rather than only in-house imitations.  
For social enterprises and development programs: treat a global flavor moment as a market opening. Cooperatives of pepper growers or beekeepers in the Global South can be linked to premium "swicy" demand, capturing more value locally through certification, storytelling, and shortened supply chains. Culinary-training nonprofits can use popular flavors to teach entrepreneurship to refugees and low-income cooks whose heritage cuisines are suddenly in vogue.  
For institutions — schools, hospitals, workplace canteens: use gateway flavors to make healthy, plant-forward food genuinely appealing. A little sweet heat can help vegetables and legumes win over skeptical eaters, advancing public-health and sustainability goals without sacrificing pleasure. For eaters and creators: be a curious, informed consumer. Ask where a flavor comes from, buy from the originators, and celebrate the people behind the trend, not just the trend itself.  
The broader lesson is that taste is never neutral. A flavor that "everyone is obsessed with" is also a distribution channel for economic opportunity, cultural respect, and ecological impact. Designed thoughtfully, swicy can be a small, delicious act of inclusion — proof that social innovation sometimes arrives one balanced, sweet-hot bite at a time.

### **中文版**

**標題：不只是菜單流行——「Swicy」甜辣風潮揭示的包容式飲食創新配方**  
**引言。**「Swicy」──甜（sweet）與辣（spicy）的合體字──已醞釀多年，而 2026 年是它徹底躍入主流的一年。淋在炸雞與披薩上的辣蜂蜜只是起點；如今這股風潮已延伸出果香、煙燻、酸香、綿密等變化，靈感取自世界各地的料理。調查指出，如今每四位消費者就有三位認為多數食物「帶點辣更好吃」，超過半數的美國食客想嘗試不尋常的風味搭配。在美味的表象之下，藏著一則關於文化交流、小型生產者經濟，以及「共享的味覺」如何開啟──或悄悄挪用──他人文化的故事。  
**從社會創新的角度看。**乍看之下，一種味道談不上社會創新。但飲食潮流是文化、生計與價值觀的強力載體，而「swicy」之所以值得剖析，正因為它的基因本就跨文化。甜辣組合古老且遍布全球：韓國的辣椒醬配蜂蜜、墨西哥沾水果的 chamoy 與辣鹽 tajín、泰式 nam jim 沾醬、四川的甜辣、西非的 suya、加勒比海的 jerk。西方的「swicy」熱潮，實質上是主流市場終於追上了離散族群與原住民社群世代打磨的風味系統。這既是機會，也是責任。  
觀察其創新動能：其一，swicy 是**小型生產者的低門檻平台**：許多辣蜂蜜、辣油品牌是家庭或移民經營的微型品牌，藉這股潮流從農夫市集擴張到全國通路，有時透過與大品牌合作。一場風味運動，因而可能成為創業的入口匝道。其二，swicy 展現了**「入門辣」的力量**：因為甜能平衡辣，它是把帳篷撐大而非縮小，邀請怕辣的人踏入更廣闊的辣味世界，進而接觸陌生的料理。食物能成為降低文化戒心的橋樑。其三，辣椒與蜂蜜**具農業意義**：需求上升可支持辣椒農，更重要的是支撐依賴授粉的養蜂業──提醒我們，餐盤上流行什麼，會一路回響到生態系與鄉村經濟。  
然而，同一股潮流也可能走偏。當大企業把一種味道從其起源故事中剝離、重新命名、獨占利潤，創造它的社群便被抹除且未獲合理報酬──這正是「料理挪用」的常見劇本。需求暴增也可能誘發不永續的採購、農業中的剝削勞動，以及只模仿「swicy」味卻毫無營養、充滿添加物的均質化產品。社會價值不在流行語本身，而在於「誰受益、誰被看重」。  
**啟示與可行的行動建議。**對主廚、品牌與零售商而言：實踐「署名與公平」。指出料理所借鑑的菜系與社群，透過公平貿易或直接貿易，向原創的農民與職人採購招牌食材，並與這些社群「共創」產品，而不只是「受其啟發」。把貨架空間與行銷資源，保留給小型與離散族群經營的生產者，而非只推自家的模仿品。  
對社會企業與發展計畫而言：把全球風味時刻視為市場開口。全球南方的辣椒農或養蜂人合作社，可以對接高端「swicy」需求，透過認證、故事行銷與縮短供應鏈，把更多價值留在在地。餐飲培訓的非營利組織，也可運用當紅風味，向難民與低收入廚師傳授創業技能──他們的家鄉味，正好一夕爆紅。  
對學校、醫院、職場餐廳等機構而言：善用「入門風味」，讓健康、以植物為主的餐點真正吸引人。一點甜辣，能讓蔬菜與豆類贏得挑嘴者的青睞，在不犧牲愉悅感的前提下，推進公共健康與永續目標。對食客與創作者而言：當一位好奇而有意識的消費者。追問味道從何而來，向原創者購買，並頌揚潮流背後的人，而不只是潮流本身。  
更宏觀的啟示是：味覺從來不中立。一種「所有人都著迷」的味道，同時也是經濟機會、文化尊重與生態影響的通路。若用心設計，swicy 可以是一次微小而美味的包容行動──證明社會創新，有時就從一口平衡的甜辣開始。

### **English Version**

**Title: The Great Meme Reset — What "2026 Is the New 2016" Tells Us About Rebuilding a Healthier Internet**  
**Introduction.** This year, a striking cultural movement has swept social media: the declaration that "2026 is the new 2016." Under hashtags generating hundreds of millions of posts, users — led by Gen Z and even Gen Alpha — are reviving the aesthetics and vibes of a decade ago: grainy mirror selfies, disappearing Snapchat-style stories, imperfect feeds, and "unserious" memes. Framed by some as a "Great Meme Reset," the movement is an explicit rebellion against AI-generated "slop," deepfakes, engagement farming, and the hyper-polished, monetized brainrot that now fills feeds. It is nostalgia, yes — but it is also a grassroots referendum on the kind of internet people want to live in.  
**A social innovation lens.** Social innovation includes not only new organizations and products but new social norms and collective behaviors that address shared problems. The "2016 reset" is a bottom-up normative innovation: millions of people, without any central coordinator, are collectively renegotiating the rules of online life. The problem they are naming is real and well-documented — a mental-health toll from comparison culture, algorithmic manipulation, and, increasingly, an epistemic crisis as AI-generated content erodes trust in what we see. Their proposed "solution" is cultural: deliberately valuing imperfection, ephemerality, authenticity, and human-made humor over algorithmic perfection.  
Several dynamics make this socially significant. First, it is a form of **digital self-governance**: rather than waiting for platforms or regulators, users are prototyping healthier norms — posting messily, curating less, logging off more, embracing offline living and "digital detox." Second, it is an **authenticity signal in an AI age**: choosing visibly human, imperfect content becomes a way to assert "a real person made this," a quiet but important act as synthetic media proliferates. Third, it doubles as **media-literacy education**: by naming "AI slop" and rejecting engineered virality, participants are teaching each other to see the machinery behind their feeds — a civic skill, not just an aesthetic preference.  
But nostalgia has limits and risks. Romanticizing 2016 can whitewash that era's own harms — misinformation, cyberbullying, filter-driven insecurity were all present. Nostalgia can become mere aesthetic consumption, another trend to be commodified (brands are already selling "2016-core"), rather than structural change. And retreat into the past is not the same as building a better future; escapism can substitute for the harder work of demanding accountable platform design and AI transparency.  
**Insights and actionable recommendations.** For educators and parents: treat this trend as a teachable moment for digital and media literacy. Use students' own enthusiasm for "spotting AI slop" to build critical skills — how algorithms shape attention, how to verify sources, how to design a healthier media diet. The movement shows young people already sense the problem; education can turn instinct into capability.  
For platform designers and technologists: listen to the signal. Users are asking for features that reward authenticity and wellbeing over raw engagement — chronological options, "quiet" modes, clear AI-content labeling, ephemerality by default, and friction against dark patterns. There is product-market fit here for humane design and for tools that reliably verify human-created content and disclose synthetic media.  
For civil society and policymakers: channel this cultural energy toward durable change. Support content-provenance standards and AI-labeling policies, digital-wellbeing curricula, and "third places" online and offline that foster genuine connection. For creators and everyday users: move from aesthetic to practice. Beyond posting a grainy selfie, protect attention deliberately, support human-made work, and model the healthier norms you want normalized.  
The deepest insight is hopeful: culture can push back. When millions independently decide the current internet is unhealthy and begin acting differently, they demonstrate that our digital environment is not fixed but designed — and therefore redesignable. "2026 is the new 2016" is, at its best, not a retreat but a rehearsal: a mass, playful experiment in imagining, and beginning to build, an internet worth spending our lives on.

### **中文版**

**標題：一場「迷因大重置」——「2026 是新的 2016」對重建健康網路的啟示**  
**引言。**今年，一股引人注目的文化運動席捲社群媒體：人們宣告「2026 是新的 2016」。在累積數億則貼文的主題標籤下，以 Z 世代、甚至 Alpha 世代為首的使用者，正重拾十年前的美學與氛圍：顆粒感的鏡子自拍、閱後即焚式的限時動態、不完美的動態牆，以及「不正經」的迷因。有人稱之為「迷因大重置」（Great Meme Reset），這場運動明確反抗 AI 生成的「內容廢料」（slop）、深偽、互動農場，以及如今充斥版面、被過度包裝與變現的「腦腐」內容。這是懷舊，沒錯──但同時也是一場由下而上、關於「我們想住在什麼樣的網路裡」的全民公投。  
**從社會創新的角度看。**社會創新不只包含新組織與新產品，也包含能回應共同問題的新社會規範與集體行為。這場「2016 重置」正是一種由下而上的規範性創新：數百萬人在沒有任何中央協調者的情況下，集體重新協商網路生活的規則。他們指認的問題真實且早有實證──比較文化、演算法操弄帶來的心理健康代價，以及隨著 AI 生成內容侵蝕「眼見為憑」的信任、日益嚴重的認識論危機。他們提出的「解方」是文化性的：刻意推崇不完美、短暫性、真實感，以及由人創作的幽默，勝過演算法式的完美。  
有幾項動能使其具社會意義。其一，這是一種**數位自治**：使用者不再枯等平台或監管者，而是自行原型測試更健康的規範──隨意地貼、少一點精修、多一點登出，擁抱線下生活與「數位排毒」。其二，這是**AI 時代的真實性訊號**：刻意選擇明顯出自人手、帶著瑕疵的內容，成了宣示「這是真人做的」的方式；在合成媒體氾濫之際，這是低調卻重要的行動。其三，它也兼具**媒體素養教育**的功能：透過指認「AI 廢料」、拒絕被操作的爆紅，參與者正互相教會彼此看見版面背後的機制──這是一種公民能力，而不只是美學偏好。  
但懷舊有其極限與風險。美化 2016 年，可能粉飾那個年代自身的傷害──假訊息、網路霸凌、濾鏡驅動的自卑，當年一樣存在。懷舊也可能淪為單純的美學消費，成為另一個被商品化的潮流（品牌早已在販售「2016 風」），而非結構性改變。而退回過去，並不等於打造更好的未來；逃避，可能取代了那項更艱難的工作──要求平台設計負起責任、要求 AI 透明。  
**啟示與可行的行動建議。**對教育者與家長而言：把這股潮流當成數位與媒體素養的教學契機。順著學生「揪出 AI 廢料」的熱情，建立批判能力──演算法如何形塑注意力、如何查核來源、如何設計更健康的資訊飲食。這場運動顯示年輕人早已感知到問題；教育能把直覺轉化為能力。  
對平台設計者與技術人而言：聽見這個訊號。使用者要求的是能獎勵真實與福祉、而非一味追求互動的功能──時序排列選項、「安靜」模式、清楚的 AI 內容標示、預設的短暫性，以及對「暗黑模式」設計的摩擦阻力。以人為本的設計，以及能可靠驗證人類創作、揭露合成媒體的工具，這裡有明確的「產品市場契合度」。  
對公民社會與決策者而言：把這股文化能量導向持久的改變。支持內容來源溯源標準與 AI 標示政策、數位福祉課程，以及線上線下能培養真實連結的「第三空間」。對創作者與一般使用者而言：從美學走向實踐。除了貼一張顆粒感自拍，更要有意識地守護注意力、支持人類創作的作品，並親自示範你希望被視為常態的健康規範。  
最深層的啟示令人懷抱希望：文化是可以反推的。當數百萬人各自判定當前的網路並不健康、並開始改變行為，他們證明了：我們的數位環境並非天生如此，而是被「設計」出來的──因此也可以被「重新設計」。「2026 是新的 2016」在最好的意義上，不是撤退，而是一場排練：一次大規模、帶著玩心的實驗，去想像、並著手打造一個值得我們投入人生的網路。
