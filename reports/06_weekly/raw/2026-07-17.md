**Topic 1（食）— The Convenience-Store Breakfast Boom**

## **English Draft**

### **Title: The 7-Eleven Breakfast That Ate the Internet — What Asia's Convenience-Store Craze Teaches Us About Feeding Cities Well**

### **Introduction**

This week one of the internet's quietest but stickiest lifestyle stories had nothing to do with a Michelin star. It was a triangle of gimbap, a carton of banana milk, and a cream bun photographed at 6 a.m. under fluorescent light. "Come with me to eat breakfast at a Korean convenience store" videos have become a genuine tourism driver, and the numbers behind the meme are staggering: foreign sales at Korea's major chains jumped year on year — CU up 101.2%, GS25 up 74.2%, 7-Eleven up 60%, and Emart24 up 38% — concentrated in tourist districts like Myeong-dong, Hongdae and Gwanghwamun. What looks like a food fad is actually a story about how modern cities distribute the most basic thing of all: an affordable, dignified meal.

### **The Social-Innovation Lens**

Social innovation is usually defined as new solutions that meet social needs more effectively than existing alternatives while creating new social relationships and capabilities. Seen this way, the Asian convenience store (CVS) is one of the most under-appreciated pieces of social infrastructure of the last two decades — and the viral moment is a chance to make that visible.  
First, the CVS solves a real equity problem: the "food-access gap." In many cities, a hot, safe, sub-US\$3 breakfast eaten at any hour is a privilege of neighbourhoods with the right restaurants and the right opening times. A 24-hour store with ready-to-eat meals flattens that gap for shift workers, students, carers and travellers alike. It is, quietly, a form of universal-basic-nutrition infrastructure delivered through private retail.  
Second, the trend reframes the store as an *experiential and social space*, not just a transaction. Tourists are not merely buying banana milk; they are assembling their own combinations, filming them, and sharing them — turning a logistics network into a participatory cultural platform. This "co-creation" is the hallmark of contemporary social innovation: value is produced *with* users, not just delivered *to* them.  
Third, and less comfortably, the boom exposes trade-offs. Ready meals mean packaging waste, ultra-processed ingredients, and pressure on franchise workers who staff those 24-hour counters on thin margins. A socially innovative response does not simply celebrate the trend; it asks how to keep the accessibility while fixing the externalities.

### **Actionable Takeaways**

> * **For city governments:** Treat convenience stores as designated "food-resilience nodes." Offer small subsidies or tax relief to chains that stock a rotating set of affordable, nutritionally-balanced "city meals," and that serve as emergency supply points during heatwaves, floods or transit failures.  
> * **For retailers:** Launch a low-cost "healthy breakfast bundle" priced for students and the elderly, paired with a reusable-packaging deposit scheme. Publish the calorie/sodium profile openly — turning transparency into a competitive advantage.  
> * **For social entrepreneurs:** Build "surplus-to-community" pipelines that redirect near-expiry ready meals to food banks via an app, converting waste into welfare while protecting store margins.  
> * **For travellers and creators:** Use the reach of the trend to spotlight *local* convenience cultures elsewhere — night-market stalls, sari-sari stores, corner bodegas — so the affection for accessible food strengthens local economies rather than one global brand.

The lesson of the viral breakfast is deceptively deep: the most inclusive innovations are often the least glamorous. A well-stocked corner store, open at dawn, is social policy disguised as convenience — and it deserves to be designed, not left to accident.

## **繁體中文初稿**

### **標題：吃掉整個網路的超商早餐——亞洲便利商店熱潮，如何教我們把「餵飽一座城市」做好**

### **引言**

本週互聯網上最安靜、卻最「黏」的一則生活熱點，和米其林星星毫無關係。它是一顆三角飯糰、一瓶香蕉牛奶，以及清晨六點在日光燈下被拍下的一個奶油麵包。「跟我去韓國便利商店吃早餐」這類影片已成為真實的旅遊驅動力，而迷因背後的數字相當驚人：韓國各大超商的外國人銷售額年增幅——CU 成長 101.2%、GS25 成長 74.2%、7-Eleven 成長 60%、Emart24 成長 38%——並集中在明洞、弘大、光化門等觀光熱區。看似一場美食流行，實則是關於現代城市如何分配「最基本之物」——一份平價而有尊嚴的餐點——的故事。

### **社會創新角度分析**

社會創新通常被定義為：以比既有方案更有效的方式滿足社會需求，同時創造新的社會關係與能力的解方。以此觀之，亞洲便利商店（CVS）是過去二十年間最被低估的一項「社會基礎設施」，而這波爆紅正是讓它「被看見」的契機。  
第一，超商解決了真實的平權問題——「食物近用落差（food-access gap）」。在許多城市，一份熱騰騰、安全、低於新台幣百元、且不分時段都能取得的早餐，其實是「擁有對的餐廳與對的營業時間」之社區的特權。一家二十四小時、供應即食餐點的門市，為輪班工作者、學生、照顧者與旅人一同抹平了這道落差。它安靜地以民間零售之姿，遞送了一種近似「普及基本營養」的基礎設施。  
第二，這股趨勢把門市重新定義為一個*體驗與社交空間*，而非單純的交易點。旅客不只是買香蕉牛奶，他們自行搭配組合、拍攝、分享——把一張物流網路轉化為一個「可參與的文化平台」。這種「共同創造（co-creation）」正是當代社會創新的標誌：價值是「與」使用者一起生產，而非只是單向「遞送」給使用者。  
第三，較不舒服的是，這波熱潮也暴露了取捨。即食餐意味著包裝廢棄物、超加工成分，以及那些以微薄利潤支撐二十四小時櫃台的加盟店員工所承受的壓力。一個具社會創新精神的回應，不會只是替趨勢喝采，而會追問：如何在保留可近性的同時，修補這些外部成本。

### **可行的啟示與行動建議**

> * **對城市政府：**將便利商店視為指定的「食物韌性節點」。對願意常態供應一組平價、營養均衡「城市餐」，並在熱浪、洪水或交通中斷時充當緊急補給點的連鎖業者，提供小額補貼或稅賦減免。  
> * **對零售業者：**推出為學生與長者定價的低價「健康早餐組合」，並搭配可重複使用包裝的押金回收機制；公開揭露熱量與鈉含量，把「透明」變成競爭優勢。  
> * **對社會創業者：**建立「剩食轉社區」的管道，透過 App 把接近效期的即食餐導向食物銀行，在保護門市利潤的同時，把浪費轉化為福利。  
> * **對旅人與創作者：**善用這股趨勢的擴散力，去聚焦「在地的」便利飲食文化——夜市攤、菲律賓 sari-sari 雜貨店、街角小舖——讓人們對「可近之食」的喜愛去強化在地經濟，而非只壯大單一全球品牌。

這則爆紅早餐的啟示比表面更深：最具包容性的創新，往往最不光鮮。一家貨架充足、天亮即開的街角小店，是一項偽裝成「便利」的社會政策——它值得被「設計」，而非任由偶然決定。

**Topic 2（行）— From Over-Tourism to Regenerative Travel**

## **English Draft**

### **Title: When the Algorithm Books the Trip — Turning the Over-Tourism Backlash into Regenerative Travel**

### **Introduction**

This week the travel conversation online split into two loud halves. On one side, TikTok and Instagram kept funnelling crowds to a handful of "must-see" viral spots — with destinations such as Isola Sacra in Italy, the Jungfrau region in Switzerland, Montmartre in Paris and Glacier National Park flagged for relief because their ecosystems and neighbourhoods are buckling under trend-driven surges. On the other side, a fast-growing counter-movement — slow travel, community-based tourism and "regenerative" travel — is telling people to stay longer, tread lighter and give back more. Gen Z and Millennials are leading it, with roughly 31% and 29% respectively saying they have joined tours that teach about local and Indigenous cultures. The same feed that causes the problem is now marketing the cure.

### **The Social-Innovation Lens**

Over-tourism is a classic "tragedy of the commons": individually rational selfies produce a collectively irrational outcome — displaced residents, priced-out housing, eroded trails and hollowed-out town centres. Social innovation matters here because the fix is not merely technological or regulatory; it requires new relationships between visitors, residents and place.  
The most interesting shift is from *sustainable* to *regenerative* travel. Sustainability aims to "do less harm"; regeneration aims to leave a place measurably better — restoring habitats, funding local guides, reviving crafts. That is a genuine reframing of the value chain: the traveller stops being a consumer of a destination and becomes a temporary steward of it. Community-based tourism, where locals own and run the experience and keep the revenue, turns tourism from extraction into shared enterprise.  
There is also a distributive innovation hiding in "slow travel." Longer stays in fewer places spread spending beyond the three viral photo-spots into the surrounding neighbourhoods, small guesthouses and off-peak seasons — smoothing the brutal boom-and-bust that wrecks local economies and ecologies. And there is a governance innovation emerging: dynamic visitor caps, timed-entry booking, and tourist taxes ring-fenced for the very neighbourhoods bearing the load.  
The honest tension is that virality and carrying capacity are fundamentally at odds. A place that "goes viral" can be loved to death in a single season. The social-innovation task is to redesign the incentives of the platforms, the pricing of access, and the stories we tell about what a "good trip" even is.

### **Actionable Takeaways**

> * **For destination authorities:** Replace blunt bans with smart demand-shaping — timed-entry, dynamic pricing, and a legally ring-fenced tourism levy that funds housing, transit and conservation in the affected districts, with spending published transparently.  
> * **For platforms and creators:** Add "carrying-capacity" context to viral location tags, and actively promote lesser-known alternatives nearby. Creators can adopt a "leave-it-better" norm: pair every viral spot post with a local business or restoration project to support.  
> * **For tour operators:** Shift to community-owned models where at least a majority of revenue stays local; publish a simple "regeneration report" (trees planted, guides employed, habitats restored) as a standard product label.  
> * **For travellers:** Adopt the "one place, one week, give something back" heuristic — stay longer, spend in the neighbourhood, join a community-led experience, and choose shoulder-season dates.

The deeper insight is that the algorithm is now a tourism planner whether we like it or not. Social innovation's job is to make sure the most-shared trip and the most-regenerative trip become the same trip.

## **繁體中文初稿**

### **標題：當演算法幫你訂了行程——把「過度旅遊」反彈化為再生式旅行**

### **引言**

本週線上的旅遊話題分裂成大聲的兩半。一邊，TikTok 與 Instagram 持續把人潮導向少數「必看」的爆紅景點——義大利的 Isola Sacra、瑞士少女峰（Jungfrau）地區、巴黎蒙馬特、美國冰川國家公園等地，都因生態與社區在趨勢驅動的暴衝人流下不堪負荷而被列為需要「紓解」的對象。另一邊，一股快速壯大的反向運動——慢旅行、社區型旅遊與「再生式（regenerative）」旅行——正呼籲人們待久一點、踩得輕一點、回饋多一點。這股潮流由 Z 世代與千禧世代領軍，各約有 31% 與 29% 表示曾參加過學習在地與原住民文化的行程。同一條餵養問題的資訊流，如今也在行銷解方。

### **社會創新角度分析**

過度旅遊是典型的「公地悲劇」：個別理性的自拍，累積出集體非理性的結果——居民被排擠、房價被推高、步道被侵蝕、城鎮中心被掏空。社會創新在此之所以關鍵，是因為解方不只是技術或法規問題，它需要在旅客、居民與地方之間，建立起新的關係。  
最值得注意的轉變，是從「永續（sustainable）」走向「再生（regenerative）」旅行。永續力求「減少傷害」；再生則力求讓一個地方變得可被衡量地更好——復育棲地、資助在地導覽、重振工藝。這是對價值鏈的真正重構：旅人不再是目的地的「消費者」，而成為它一段時間的「守護者」。在社區型旅遊中，由在地人擁有並經營體驗、並留下營收，讓旅遊從「榨取」轉為「共享的事業」。  
「慢旅行」中也藏著一項「分配式創新」。在更少的地方待更久，能讓消費從那三個爆紅打卡點，擴散到周邊的街區、小型民宿與淡季時段，撫平那種摧毀在地經濟與生態的劇烈榮枯循環。此外，一種「治理創新」也正浮現：動態遊客總量管制、分時預約入場，以及專款專用、回饋給承受衝擊之社區的觀光稅。  
誠實地說，張力在於「爆紅」與「承載量」根本相互矛盾。一個「爆紅」之地，可能在一個旺季內就被愛到崩壞。社會創新的任務，是重新設計平台的誘因、近用的定價，以及我們對「一趟好旅程」究竟為何的敘事。

### **可行的啟示與行動建議**

> * **對目的地主管機關：**以「聰明的需求塑形」取代粗暴的禁令——分時入場、動態定價，並設立法定專款專用的觀光稅，用於受衝擊地區的住宅、交通與保育，且支出公開透明。  
> * **對平台與創作者：**在爆紅地標標籤上加註「承載量」脈絡，並主動推廣鄰近較不知名的替代選項。創作者可採行「讓它更好」的準則：每則爆紅景點貼文，都搭配一個值得支持的在地商家或復育計畫。  
> * **對旅遊業者：**轉向社區共有模式，讓至少過半營收留在地方；並把簡明的「再生報告」（植樹數、雇用導覽人數、復育棲地）當成標準的產品標示。  
> * **對旅人：**採用「一地、一週、回饋一點」的原則——待久一點、在街區內消費、參加社區主導的體驗、並選擇淡旺季之間的日期。

更深一層的洞見是：無論我們喜不喜歡，演算法如今已是一位旅遊規劃師。社會創新的工作，就是讓「最多人分享的旅程」與「最具再生力的旅程」，成為同一趟旅程。

**Topic 3（樂）— The 2026 World Cup Final as a Social Platform**

## **English Draft**

### **Title: One Million Trees and One Final Whistle — Can the 2026 World Cup Leave More Than a Carbon Footprint?**

### **Introduction**

As the internet counts down to the FIFA World Cup 2026 final on 19 July at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey, the tournament has become the week's biggest shared "樂" (leisure) moment — a rare event that stops arguments and unites feeds across the United States, Mexico and Canada. But this World Cup carries a double headline. It is the first jointly hosted by three nations across 16 cities and 104 matches, and it is projected to be one of the highest-emitting in history, with estimates of at least 9 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent. Alongside that footprint, FIFA and host cities are running some of the largest community programmes ever attached to the event: a pledge to plant one million trees with the Arbor Day Foundation, more than 12,000 community trees across all 16 host cities, and public-transit expansions meant to outlast the tournament. A month of football is also a month-long stress test of whether mega-events can do social good at scale.

### **The Social-Innovation Lens**

Mega-events are the ultimate double-edged sword of social innovation. They concentrate money, attention and civic ambition into a short window — which can either be squandered on white-elephant stadiums or leveraged into lasting public value. The 2026 tournament is a live experiment in the second path.  
The most durable innovations here are the ones designed to survive 20 July. Transit expansions built to move fans are, in effect, permanent mobility infrastructure for residents — a classic case of "piggybacking" long-term public goods onto a short-term spectacle. Community tree-planting in parks, school campuses and trail systems converts global sponsorship money into hyper-local, neighbourhood-scale environmental assets. These are examples of "legacy-by-design": planning the afterlife of the event before the first kick-off.  
Football's real social-innovation superpower, though, is convening. No policy campaign can assemble the shared attention of billions the way a World Cup can. That attention is a form of civic capital that can be pointed at inclusion (accessible stadiums, women's football investment, refugee and youth programmes) or wasted. The genuine test of social innovation is whether host cities use the platform to build *capabilities* — local coaching, grassroots pitches, small-business supply chains — rather than just stage a show.  
The uncomfortable truth remains the emissions. Twelve thousand trees do not offset nine million tonnes of carbon, and framing them as if they do would be greenwashing. An honest social-innovation stance treats the tree-planting and transit as necessary-but-insufficient, and pushes for the harder structural changes: fewer, closer venues; renewable-powered stadiums; and rail-first fan travel in future editions.

### **Actionable Takeaways**

> * **For host cities:** Lock in "legacy covenants" now — binding commitments that transit lines, accessible facilities and community pitches remain funded and public for at least a decade after the final.  
> * **For FIFA and sponsors:** Publish a transparent, third-party-audited emissions ledger and fund *structural* decarbonisation (renewable stadium power, regional match clustering) rather than symbolic offsets alone.  
> * **For local organisations:** Use the attention window to launch grassroots programmes — girls' football leagues, coaching qualifications for young people, and supplier contracts reserved for local small businesses and social enterprises.  
> * **For fans:** Choose rail and shared transit to matches, support host-city community funds, and back clubs and initiatives that keep the excitement alive locally after the trophy is lifted.

The final whistle on 19 July will end the football. Whether it also plants a lasting public good — or just a carbon bill — is a design choice being made right now. That choice is exactly where social innovation lives.

## **繁體中文初稿**

### **標題：一百萬棵樹與一聲終場哨——2026 世界盃能留下的，能否不只是碳足跡？**

### **引言**

當網路倒數著 7 月 19 日於紐澤西大都會人壽球場（MetLife Stadium）舉行的 2026 世界盃足球賽決賽，這場賽事已成為本週最大的共享「樂」時刻——一個能讓爭吵停下、讓美國、墨西哥與加拿大的資訊流一同凝聚的罕見事件。但這屆世界盃帶著雙重標題。它是首屆由三國、橫跨 16 座城市、104 場比賽共同主辦的賽事；同時也被預估將成為史上碳排最高的賽事之一，估計至少排放 900 萬公噸二氧化碳當量。在這道足跡之外，國際足總（FIFA）與主辦城市也推動了史上規模數一數二的社區計畫：與 Arbor Day 基金會合作承諾種下一百萬棵樹、在全部 16 座主辦城市植下超過 12,000 棵社區樹木，以及意在延續到賽事之後的大眾運輸擴建。一個月的足球，同時也是一場長達一個月、檢驗「大型賽事能否大規模行社會善」的壓力測試。

### **社會創新角度分析**

大型賽事是社會創新裡的終極雙面刃。它把金錢、注意力與公民企圖心，濃縮進一段極短的時窗——這既可能被浪費在「白象工程」的閒置球場上，也可能被槓桿成長久的公共價值。2026 世界盃，正是後者這條路徑的一場即時實驗。  
此處最耐久的創新，是那些「被設計成能撐過 7 月 20 日」之後的項目。為了運送球迷而興建的運輸擴建，實質上就是留給居民的永久移動基礎設施——這是把「長期公共財」搭載於「短期盛事」之上的經典手法。在公園、校園與步道系統的社區植樹，則把全球贊助資金，轉化為超在地、街區尺度的環境資產。這些都是「以設計預留遺產（legacy-by-design）」的範例：在第一聲開球哨之前，就先規劃好賽事的「來世」。
