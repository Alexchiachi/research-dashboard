### **English Version**

#### **When the Phones Go Quiet: What the Viral School Phone-Ban Movement Teaches Us About Social Innovation**

**Introduction.** This week the internet could not stop talking about an unlikely protagonist: the classroom without smartphones. A high-school teacher's TikTok describing the first phone-free day of the year drew more than two million views, while fresh policies landed almost simultaneously across jurisdictions — Michigan began its first year under a statewide student cellphone ban on 30 September, the Cayman Islands published a new "Safe School" mobile-phone policy on 25 September, and a widely shared Slate essay from a New Jersey teacher captured the strange, hopeful awkwardness of a building that suddenly went analog. By early 2026, 35 U.S. states and Washington, D.C. had enacted or signed restrictions. What makes this a story worth reading through a social-innovation lens is not the novelty of a rule, but the speed at which a quiet, low-cost intervention has spread — and what it reveals about how societies repair shared problems.  
**The social-innovation angle.** Social innovation is usually defined as a novel solution to a social problem that is more effective, efficient, or just than existing approaches, and whose value accrues primarily to society rather than to private shareholders. The phone-free classroom fits that definition almost perfectly, and it is instructive precisely because it is so unglamorous. There is no app, no venture round, no hardware. The "technology" is a rule plus a cheap locking pouch or a labelled wall-caddy. Yet it targets a genuine collective-action failure. Individual families cannot solve adolescent attention fragmentation alone: a teenager whose peers are all on their phones pays a social cost for being the only one disconnected. The ban works not by moralizing at individuals but by changing the default for everyone at once — the classic structure of a social innovation that resolves a coordination trap. When the whole room is offline, no one is singled out, and the behaviour becomes sustainable rather than heroic.  
Three features make the movement a textbook case. First, it is evidence-seeking rather than purely ideological. A National Bureau of Economic Research working paper and district-level data are being used to test claims about behaviour and achievement, meaning the innovation carries its own feedback loop. Second, it is diffusing through a hybrid of bottom-up and top-down channels: a single teacher's video supplies the emotional proof-of-concept, while state legislation supplies the scale. That pairing — viral narrative plus policy infrastructure — is exactly how social innovations cross the chasm from pilot to norm. Third, it redistributes a scarce public good: student attention. In an attention economy engineered to extract it, a school that reclaims six hours a day is effectively performing a small act of redistribution, returning cognitive bandwidth to the children who need it most and, research suggests, disproportionately helping lower-achieving and more distractible students.  
But a mature social-innovation reading also flags the risks. The first is substitution. As one commentary noted, when phones leave the desk, school-issued laptops and AI chatbots may quietly fill the screen-time gap, so a ban on one device can become a loophole for another. A genuine innovation must address the underlying need — focused, relational learning — not merely the symbolic object. The second risk is equity in reverse: phones are also lifelines for students managing diabetes, translating for immigrant parents, or coordinating unsafe home situations. Well-designed policies carve out medical and accessibility exceptions; poorly designed ones punish the vulnerable. The third is the danger of treating a ban as a finished solution rather than as the opening of space. The real prize is what fills the reclaimed time — conversation, boredom, play, repair of the peer relationships that screens had thinned.  
**Actionable implications.** For educators and administrators: pair the restriction with a deliberate plan for the freed hours — structured unstructured time, clubs, outdoor breaks — and publish simple before/after indicators (referrals, participation, wellbeing surveys) so the community can see the innovation working or failing honestly. For policymakers: write exceptions for health, disability and caregiving into the statute from day one, and regulate the substitution risk by setting norms for school devices and classroom AI alongside the phone rule. For parents and civil society: treat the school ban as a template for a household or neighbourhood version — a shared, agreed default is far easier to sustain than a parent-versus-child standoff. For social innovators more broadly: the lesson is that the highest-leverage interventions are often not new products but new defaults, spread by a credible story and anchored by legitimate institutions. The phone-free classroom is a reminder that sometimes the most radical innovation is subtraction — the deliberate, collective choice to remove something, and to protect the human capacity that rushes back in to fill the silence.

### **中文版**

#### **當教室安靜下來：爆紅的校園禁手機運動，教會我們的社會創新一課**

**引言。**本週互聯網的主角出人意料——是一間「沒有手機」的教室。一位高中教師在 TikTok 上描述開學後第一個「無手機日」的影片，衝破兩百萬次觀看；與此同時，多地政策幾乎同步落地：密西根州在 9 月 30 日迎來全州學生禁用手機的首個學年，開曼群島於 9 月 25 日公布全新的「安全校園」手機政策，而一位紐澤西教師在《Slate》的文章，生動捕捉了整棟校舍突然「回到類比時代」那種既尷尬又充滿希望的氛圍。截至 2026 年初，美國已有 35 州與華盛頓特區立法或簽署相關限制。值得以社會創新角度細讀的，不是「禁令」本身的新奇，而是這個低成本、安靜的介入擴散之快，以及它揭示了社會如何修補共同難題。  
**社會創新角度分析。**社會創新通常被定義為：對某個社會問題提出比既有做法更有效、更有效率或更公義的新解方，而其價值主要歸於社會而非私人股東。無手機教室幾乎完美符合這個定義，而它之所以極具啟發性，恰恰在於它一點都不「炫」。沒有 App、沒有募資輪、沒有硬體。它的「科技」只是一條規則，加上一個廉價的收納袋或貼了姓名的牆上口袋格。然而它精準命中了一個真正的集體行動失靈：個別家庭無法單獨解決青少年注意力碎片化——當同儕都在滑手機，唯一「離線」的孩子要付出社交代價。禁令的高明之處，不是對個人說教，而是「一次為所有人改變預設值」，這正是化解協調陷阱的典型社會創新結構。當整間教室一起離線，沒有人被針對，於是「專注」不再需要英雄式的意志力，而能可持續地維持。  
三個特徵讓這場運動成為教科書級案例。其一，它尋求證據，而非純粹意識形態：美國國家經濟研究局（NBER）的工作論文與各學區的數據，正被用來檢驗行為與學業成效的主張，意味著這項創新內建了回饋迴路。其二，它透過「由下而上」與「由上而下」的混合通道擴散：一位教師的影片提供了情感上的概念驗證，州層級立法則提供了規模化的基礎設施——「病毒式敘事＋政策基礎建設」的配對，正是社會創新從試點跨越鴻溝、成為常態的方式。其三，它重新分配了一項稀缺的公共財：學生的注意力。在一個被設計來「榨取」注意力的注意力經濟中，一所學校每天奪回六小時，等於完成了一次微型的再分配，把認知頻寬還給最需要的孩子；研究亦顯示，這對原本成績較弱、較易分心的學生助益尤其明顯。  
但成熟的社會創新視角也會標示風險。第一是「替代效應」：有評論指出，手機離開課桌後，學校配發的筆電與 AI 聊天機器人可能悄悄填補螢幕時間的空缺，於是禁掉一種裝置，反而成了另一種裝置的漏洞。真正的創新必須回應底層需求——專注而有連結的學習——而非只對付那個象徵性的物件。第二是「反向的公平問題」：手機對某些學生是生命線，用來管理糖尿病、替移民父母翻譯、或在不安全的家庭處境中求助。設計良好的政策會為醫療與無障礙需求保留例外，設計拙劣的則會懲罰弱勢。第三，是把「禁令」當成完成式、而非一個被打開的空間。真正的獎賞，是被奪回的時間裡填進了什麼——對話、無聊、玩耍，以及修復那些曾被螢幕稀釋的同儕關係。  
**可行啟示與行動建議。**給教育工作者與行政者：把「限制」與「如何運用被釋放的時間」的計畫綁在一起——有結構的自由時間、社團、戶外休息——並公開簡單的前後指標（違規通報、參與度、身心健康問卷），讓社群能誠實地看見創新究竟奏效或失敗。給政策制定者：從第一天起就把健康、身心障礙與照顧責任的例外寫進法規，並在手機規則之外，一併為學校裝置與課堂 AI 設定規範，以管理替代風險。給家長與公民社會：把校園禁令當成家庭或社區版的範本——一個共同協議的「預設值」，遠比「家長對抗孩子」的拉鋸更容易維持。給更廣義的社會創新者：最高槓桿的介入，往往不是新產品，而是新的「預設值」，靠一則可信的故事傳播、由正當的制度錨定。無手機教室提醒我們：有時最激進的創新是「減法」——刻意而集體地移除某樣東西，並守護那些在安靜重新湧回時，才得以復甦的人類能力。

**熱點二：亞洲「超商觀光」——7-Eleven 成旅客朝聖地（食／行）**

### **English Version**

#### **The 24-Hour Oasis: What "Convenience-Store Tourism" Reveals About Everyday Social Innovation**

**Introduction.** One of the week's most-shared travel stories had nothing to do with temples, beaches or Michelin stars. It was about a ham-and-cheese toastie. Across Asia, the humble convenience store has become a genuine tourist attraction: Fast Company reported that Japan's 7-Elevens are "the hottest new tourist attraction," while Thai and regional media chronicled how foreign visitors now treat a hot sandwich, a DIY drink and the fluorescent aisles of a Thai 7-Eleven as a must-do ritual. Short videos reviewing "must-try" snacks and limited-edition desserts are racking up millions of views, pulling travellers to Japan, South Korea, Thailand and Singapore just to experience the stores. It sounds trivial. But seen through a social-innovation lens, grocery tourism is a window onto something serious: how a piece of everyday infrastructure can be redesigned into shared public value.  
**The social-innovation angle.** The Asian convenience store — the Japanese *konbini*, Thailand's 7-Eleven, Taiwan's dense network of stores — is one of the most underrated social innovations of the last half-century. It began as retail, but it has quietly become civic infrastructure. In these stores you can pay utility bills and taxes, collect and send parcels, buy concert and transit tickets, withdraw cash, print documents, dispose of recycling, find a clean toilet, and shelter during a typhoon or earthquake; in Japan, designated stores are official disaster-relief supply points. This is social innovation in its truest sense: an institution that bundles dozens of fragmented public and private services into a single, trusted, walkable, 24-hour node, accessible regardless of income. The viral tourism is simply outsiders discovering what residents already knew — that the *konbini* is less a shop than a compressed welfare-and-logistics layer for the whole society.  
Why does this matter for innovators? First, because it reframes what "infrastructure" can look like. We tend to imagine public services as large, centralized and government-run. The convenience store demonstrates a distributed alternative: thousands of small, uniform, reliable nodes, dense enough that one is almost always within walking distance. That density is itself an equity intervention — it serves the carless, the elderly, shift-workers and the rural edges of cities that formal services often miss. Second, it shows the power of standardization-with-locality: the format is rigorously consistent (which is what makes it trustworthy) yet each store localizes its food and services, so the model scales without becoming sterile. Third, the tourism wave illustrates a soft-power and economic-development dynamic: Thailand's 7-Eleven culture has become a form of national branding, converting everyday life into an export and channelling tourist spending toward affordable, locally made products rather than luxury imports. That redistributive tilt — tourism money reaching ordinary supply chains — is a quietly progressive outcome.  
A critical reading must hold the tensions too. The same density that serves communities can hollow out independent grocers and family shops, concentrating retail power in a few corporate chains and homogenizing high streets. The 24-hour model historically rested on precarious, low-wage and franchise-owner labour working punishing hours; Japan's own *konbini* operators have faced reckonings over mandatory round-the-clock opening. And grocery tourism, like all tourism, can tip into spectacle — crowds filming staff and aisles, treating a working community space as a photo set. A socially innovative response does not reject the *konbini* model; it asks how to keep its public value while fixing its labour and competition externalities.  
**Actionable implications.** For cities and planners: study the convenience store as a template for "20-minute" service access — co-locate public services (bill payment, parcel lockers, health kiosks, disaster supplies) in dense, trusted, walkable nodes rather than scattering them across hard-to-reach offices. For convenience-store operators: lean into the civic role deliberately — formalize disaster-relief partnerships, accessibility, and community functions, and reform the labour model (end coerced 24-hour opening, improve franchisee and worker terms) so the social value is not built on hidden costs. For tourism boards: the Thai example shows that authentic, affordable everyday experiences can be powerful, low-carbon, high-dispersion attractions that spread spending beyond overcrowded icons — but market them with respect for the workers and residents who rely on these spaces daily. For social innovators everywhere: the *konbini* is proof that the most transformative infrastructure is often hiding in plain sight, and that bundling scattered services into one trusted, always-open node is a design pattern worth exporting far beyond retail.

### **中文版**

#### **24 小時的綠洲：「超商觀光」揭示的日常社會創新**

**引言。**本週最被瘋傳的旅遊故事之一，與寺廟、海灘或米其林星星都無關——它講的是一份火腿起司三明治。在亞洲，不起眼的便利商店已成為貨真價實的觀光景點：《Fast Company》報導日本的 7-Eleven 是「最熱門的新興觀光景點」，泰國與區域媒體則記錄了外國旅客如何把一份熱三明治、一杯自助調製的飲料，以及泰國 7-Eleven 那一排排日光燈下的貨架，當成必做的旅遊儀式。評測「必買」零食與限定甜點的短影片，動輒數百萬次觀看，吸引旅客專程飛往日本、南韓、泰國與新加坡，只為體驗這些超商。聽起來很瑣碎。但若透過社會創新的透鏡來看，「超商觀光」其實是一扇窗，映照出一件嚴肅的事：一項日常基礎設施，如何被重新設計成共享的公共價值。  
**社會創新角度分析。**亞洲的便利商店——日本的「コンビニ」、泰國的 7-Eleven、台灣綿密的超商網——是過去半世紀最被低估的社會創新之一。它起於零售，卻悄悄成為公民基礎設施。在這些店裡，你可以繳水電費與稅款、寄取包裹、買演唱會與交通票券、提領現金、列印文件、處理回收、使用乾淨廁所，甚至在颱風或地震時暫避；在日本，指定門市更是官方的防災物資供應點。這才是社會創新最真切的意義：一個把數十項零散的公共與私人服務，綑綁進單一、可信、步行可及、24 小時運作節點的制度，且不論收入高低皆可使用。瘋傳的觀光熱，不過是外來者發現了居民早已知道的事——「超商」與其說是一間店，不如說是整個社會壓縮版的「福利＋物流」層。  
這對創新者為何重要？第一，它重新定義了「基礎設施」的樣貌。我們習慣把公共服務想像成大型、集中、由政府營運。便利商店示範了一種分散式的替代方案：數以千計小型、規格一致、可靠的節點，密度高到幾乎永遠有一家在步行範圍內。這種密度本身就是一種公平介入——它服務了沒有汽車的人、長者、輪班工作者，以及正式服務常忽略的城市邊緣與鄉村。第二，它展現了「標準化結合在地化」的力量：格式嚴格一致（這正是它令人信任的原因），但每家店都在地化其食物與服務，於是模式得以規模化卻不致僵化貧乏。第三，這波觀光熱示範了軟實力與經濟發展的動態：泰國的 7-Eleven 文化已成為一種國家品牌，把日常生活轉化為輸出品，並把旅客的消費導向平價、在地製造的商品，而非奢侈進口品——這種「觀光財流向庶民供應鏈」的再分配傾向，是一個低調而進步的結果。  
但批判性的解讀也必須正視其張力。同樣的密度，既服務社群，也可能掏空獨立雜貨店與家庭小店，使零售權力集中於少數企業連鎖，並讓街景趨於同質化。24 小時模式在歷史上，往往建立在不穩定、低薪的勞動與加盟主超時工作之上；日本的超商體系本身，就曾因強制全天候營業而面臨檢討。而超商觀光，一如所有觀光，也可能淪為奇觀——人群拍攝店員與貨架，把一個仍在運作的社群空間當成攝影棚。具社會創新精神的回應，不是否定超商模式，而是追問：如何在修補其勞動與競爭外部性的同時，保住它的公共價值。  
**可行啟示與行動建議。**給城市與規劃者：把便利商店當成「20 分鐘生活圈」服務可及性的範本——將公共服務（繳費、包裹櫃、健康資訊站、防災物資）共置於密集、可信、步行可及的節點，而非散落在難以抵達的機關辦公室。給超商業者：主動擁抱公民角色——將防災合作、無障礙與社區功能制度化，並改革勞動模式（終止被迫的 24 小時營業、改善加盟主與員工條件），使社會價值不建立在隱藏成本之上。給觀光主管機關：泰國的例子顯示，真實、平價的日常體驗，可以是低碳、高分散的強大賣點，把消費分散到擁擠地標之外——但行銷時須尊重那些每日仰賴這些空間的勞工與居民。給各地社會創新者：「超商」證明了最具變革性的基礎設施，往往就藏在眾目睽睽之下；而把零散服務綑綁進一個可信、永遠營業的節點，是一種值得輸出到零售業以外的設計模式。

### **English Version**

#### **Slow Down, See More: How the "Yearncation" Rural-Escape Trend Points Toward Regenerative Social Innovation**

**Introduction.** This week travel media converged on a single idea with several names. "Yearncation" describes city-dwellers daydreaming of quaint countryside B\&Bs and a slower life before social media dictated vacations. "Tiny tourism" describes travellers choosing hyper-local experiences over crowded icons. And underneath both sits the broader movement the industry is calling slow travel — the biggest trip trend of 2026, built on longer stays, deeper cultural connection, and a shift from "seeing" a landmark to "feeling" a place. European travel forecasts point to rural retreats and historic hotels; reporting on Japan, Italy and Thailand describes a pivot away from saturated urban centres toward "slow food" corridors and agrarian hubs. The common thread is not just a mood. It is a structural response to overtourism — and that makes it fertile ground for social innovation.  
**The social-innovation angle.** Overtourism is a textbook social problem: a few honeypot destinations absorb unsustainable crowds, housing is converted to short-term rentals until locals are priced out, infrastructure strains, and the cultural experience degrades for everyone. The slow-travel movement is interesting because it attacks that problem not through a top-down cap or ban, but by redesigning demand itself. If travellers can be persuaded that a week living like a local in an overlooked village is more desirable than two rushed days in a crowded capital, then tourist flows — and the money that follows them — redistribute toward communities that mass tourism ignored. Reporting explicitly frames this as "redistributing economic gains to agrarian communities." That is a demand-side social innovation: it changes behaviour by shifting what is considered aspirational, and it harnesses a cultural trend to do work that regulation struggles to achieve.  
Seen this way, several innovation mechanisms are at play. First, value redefinition: slow travel reframes scarcity. In the old model, prestige came from access to famous places; in the new one, it comes from depth, authenticity and time — intangibles that any region can offer, which democratizes who can benefit from tourism. Second, longer stays change the economics for hosts: a traveller who lingers a week buys groceries, uses local services, learns a craft and forms relationships, circulating money through the local economy far more than a day-tripper who arrives by cruise ship and leaves. Third, the trend dovetails with regenerative and lower-carbon tourism: fewer, longer, closer trips mean fewer flights per experience and spending directed at small producers, aligning traveller desire with environmental and community resilience. The "yearncation" nostalgia even carries a wellbeing dimension — a collective yearning for digital detox and slower rhythms that connects this story back to the phone-free movement above.  
But a careful social-innovation reading warns against romanticism. The same force that revives a sleepy village can, if it succeeds too well, simply export overtourism to new places — today's hidden gem is tomorrow's overrun hotspot, its housing hollowed into holiday lets. "Authenticity" marketed to outsiders can commodify and distort the very cultures it claims to honour, turning living communities into performances. And rural tourism, badly governed, can leak its profits to outside platforms and investors rather than residents. The innovation, therefore, is not the trend by itself; it is the governance and design wrapped around it — community ownership, caps, benefit-sharing — that decides whether a village is enriched or consumed.  
**Actionable implications.** For destination communities: capture the wave on your own terms — favour locally owned stays, cooperatives and farm-stays over absentee-owned rentals; cap and stagger visitor numbers; and design experiences (craft workshops, harvests, shared meals) that keep value and decision-making local. For policymakers: use the demand shift as leverage — pair incentives for rural and off-peak travel with protections against short-term-rental housing loss, and route tourism revenue into local infrastructure and conservation. For travel businesses and platforms: build products around longer stays and genuine local partnerships, and measure success by money retained in the community, not just bookings. For travellers: treat slow travel as a practice with ethics, not just an aesthetic — go in low season, spend with small local businesses, learn before you arrive, and resist turning a living place into content. For social innovators: this trend is a reminder that one of the most powerful levers for change is desire itself. Redefining what people aspire to — from checklist spectacle to rooted, regenerative experience — can redistribute resources and repair places more durably than any rule imposed from above.

### **中文版**

#### **慢下來，看見更多：「yearncation」鄉村出走潮如何指向再生式社會創新**

**引言。**本週旅遊媒體不約而同聚焦於同一個概念，而它有好幾個名字。「Yearncation」（嚮往假期）描述都市人對於鄉間雅緻民宿、以及社群媒體尚未主宰假期前那種慢生活的憧憬；「微型觀光」（tiny tourism）則指旅客選擇超在地的體驗，而非擁擠的知名地標。而這兩者底下，是業界稱為「慢旅行」（slow travel）的更大運動——2026 年最大的旅遊趨勢，奠基於更長的停留、更深的文化連結，以及從「看見」地標轉向「感受」一個地方。歐洲旅遊預測指向鄉村度假與歷史旅館；關於日本、義大利與泰國的報導，則描述了一場從飽和的都會中心，轉向「慢食」廊帶與農業聚落的轉向。它們的共同主線不只是一種心情，而是一種對「過度觀光」的結構性回應——這正使它成為社會創新的沃土。  
**社會創新角度分析。**過度觀光是教科書級的社會問題：少數熱門景點吸納了無法永續的人潮，住宅被改成短租房直到在地人被迫遷離，基礎設施不堪負荷，文化體驗對所有人都在劣化。慢旅行運動之所以有趣，在於它攻擊這個問題的方式，不是由上而下的管制或禁令，而是「重新設計需求本身」。如果能說服旅客相信——在一個被忽略的村莊像在地人般住上一週，比在擁擠首都匆忙待兩天更令人嚮往——那麼觀光人流、以及隨之而來的金錢，就會重新分配到那些被大眾觀光忽視的社群。報導明確地把這描述為「把經濟收益重新分配給農業社群」。這是一種「需求端的社會創新」：它藉由改變「什麼才令人嚮往」來改變行為，並駕馭一股文化趨勢，去完成法規難以達成的工作。  
如此來看，有幾種創新機制正在運作。第一是「價值的重新定義」：慢旅行重新框定了稀缺性。在舊模式裡，聲望來自能否抵達名勝；在新模式裡，聲望來自深度、真實與時間——這些無形之物是任何地區都能提供的，因而讓「誰能從觀光中受益」變得更民主。第二，更長的停留改變了接待方的經濟結構：待上一週的旅客會買菜、使用在地服務、學習一門手藝、建立關係，讓金錢在地方經濟中流轉，遠勝於搭郵輪來、當天就走的過客。第三，這股趨勢與「再生式」及低碳觀光相契合：更少、更長、更近的旅程，意味著每次體驗的飛行次數更少，消費導向小型生產者，使旅人的渴望與環境及社群韌性相互對齊。而「yearncation」的懷舊，甚至帶有身心健康的面向——一種對數位排毒與慢節奏的集體嚮往，正好把這則故事與前文的「無手機運動」連結起來。  
但審慎的社會創新解讀，要警惕浪漫化。同一股讓沉睡村莊復甦的力量，若太過成功，可能只是把過度觀光輸出到新地點——今日的私房祕境，就是明日被擠爆的熱點，其住宅被掏空成度假出租屋。對外來者行銷的「真實性」，可能把它聲稱要尊崇的文化本身商品化、扭曲，使活生生的社群淪為表演。而治理不當的鄉村觀光，其利潤可能外流給外部平台與投資者，而非居民。因此，真正的創新不是趨勢本身，而是包裹在它外面的「治理與設計」——社區所有權、人數上限、利益共享——這些才決定了一個村莊是被豐富，還是被消費。  
**可行啟示與行動建議。**給目的地社群：以自己的條件承接這波浪潮——優先發展在地持有的住宿、合作社與農家民宿，而非外地人持有的出租房；對遊客數量設限並錯峰；設計能把價值與決策權留在地方的體驗（手作工坊、採收、共餐）。給政策制定者：把需求轉向當成槓桿——將鼓勵鄉村與淡季旅行的誘因，與防止短租侵蝕住宅的保護措施並行，並把觀光收益導入地方基礎建設與保育。給旅遊業者與平台：圍繞更長停留與真誠的在地夥伴關係打造產品，並以「留在社群的金錢」而非僅僅訂房量來衡量成功。給旅人：把慢旅行當成一種有倫理的實踐，而非只是一種美學——淡季出遊、向在地小商家消費、出發前先認識當地、並克制把一個活生生的地方變成「內容」。給社會創新者：這股趨勢提醒我們，最強大的改變槓桿之一，就是「渴望」本身。重新定義人們所嚮往的事物——從清單式的奇觀，轉向有根、再生的體驗——能比任何由上而下強加的規則，更持久地重新分配資源、修復地方。
