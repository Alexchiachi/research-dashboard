## **English Draft**

### **Aisles as Attractions: How "Shelf Discovery" Turns the Supermarket into a Social Innovation Engine**

#### **Introduction**

One of the most-shared lifestyle trends of the week is also one of the most ordinary places imaginable: the local grocery store. Under the label "Shelf Discovery"—also called grocery-store tourism or the "supermarket safari"—travelers are deliberately routing their trips through corner shops, mini-marts and supermarkets instead of, or before, the famous monuments. Skyscanner's 2026 travel data reports that 31% of U.S. travelers plan to explore local supermarkets on their trips this year, and 55% say they always or often visit a foreign grocery store when abroad. Social feeds have turned snack hauls in South Korea, Japan, Singapore, Bali and Vietnam into de facto travel guides. What looks like a quirky habit is, on closer inspection, a low-cost, high-meaning way of encountering a place—and a surprisingly rich case study in social innovation.

#### **The Social Innovation Angle**

Social innovation is not only about non-profits or grand programs; it is about new practices that solve a shared problem while redistributing value more fairly. Shelf Discovery does exactly that against three converging pressures: travel inflation, over-tourism, and the hollowing-out of "authentic" experience by curated content.  
**1\. It reframes affordability as access, not austerity.** Global travel has become expensive, and the industry's default answer is to sell premium exclusivity. Shelf Discovery inverts this: a €4 basket of regional snacks delivers a genuine cultural encounter at a fraction of the cost of a tasting menu. This is a grassroots response to inflation that expands who gets to travel meaningfully—students, families, and budget travelers included—rather than pricing them out.  
**2\. It redistributes tourist spending toward everyday local economies.** Conventional tourism concentrates money in a narrow band of landmark-adjacent businesses, while residents bear the congestion. When visitors shop where locals shop, spending flows to neighborhood grocers, regional food producers and small brands that rarely appear in guidebooks. It is a quiet form of economic decentralization that spreads tourism's benefits more evenly and reduces pressure on overcrowded hotspots.  
**3\. It rebuilds cultural understanding through the mundane.** A supermarket is an unfiltered index of how a society actually eats, prices, packages and lives. Reading shelves—seasonal produce, portion norms, what is cheap and what is dear—teaches cultural literacy that no staged attraction can. In an era flooded with synthetic and highly-curated content, the ordinariness is precisely the value: it is "proof of real life."  
**4\. It aligns with sustainability.** Buying local staples reduces reliance on imported, heavily packaged tourist food and supports shorter supply chains. Grocery tourism has been described as an antidote to travel inflation precisely because it is also lighter on resources.

#### **Actionable Implications**

**For cities and DMOs (destination marketing organizations):** Instead of only promoting landmarks, publish "local market trails" that guide visitors to neighborhood grocers and food halls, spreading footfall away from saturated sites and into communities that want the business. Partner with resident associations so the benefit is welcomed, not resented.  
**For small grocers and regional producers:** Add simple multilingual shelf-talkers ("try this local specialty"), accept common travel-payment methods, and create a small "visitor's basket" of signature items. Low-cost changes can convert curious tourists into a new revenue line.  
**For social enterprises and NGOs:** Use the trend to channel tourist spending toward cooperatives, refugee-run food businesses, or fair-trade local brands—turning a viral habit into inclusive economic participation.  
**For travelers:** Treat the grocery run as intentional cultural learning. Buy from independent and cooperative stores where possible, ask staff for their favorite item, and share what you learn—not just what you bought.

#### **Conclusion**

Shelf Discovery shows that meaningful innovation often hides in plain sight. By re-valuing the most ordinary of spaces, travelers have stumbled onto a model that is cheaper, more inclusive, more sustainable and more culturally honest than much of the tourism industry it quietly critiques. The opportunity for cities, businesses and social enterprises is to recognize the aisle as an attraction—and to design deliberately for the fairer distribution of value it makes possible.

### **走道即景點：「貨架探索」如何把超市變成社會創新的引擎**

#### **引言**

本週最被瘋傳的生活趨勢之一，發生在最平凡不過的地方——當地的超級市場。在「Shelf Discovery（貨架探索）」的名號下，也被稱為「超市旅遊」或「超市狩獵」，旅人們刻意把行程繞進街角小店、迷你超商與大型量販，取代或優先於知名景點。Skyscanner 2026 旅遊數據指出，今年有 31% 的美國旅客計畫在旅程中造訪當地超市，55% 表示出國時「總是或經常」逛當地雜貨店。社群上，關於韓國、日本、新加坡、峇里島與越南的零食開箱，已然成為實質上的旅遊指南。看似古怪的習慣，細看之下卻是一種低成本、高意義的城市體驗方式，更是一則出乎意料豐富的社會創新案例。

#### **社會創新角度分析**

社會創新不只關乎非營利組織或宏大計畫，它指的是「以新做法解決共同問題、同時更公平地重新分配價值」。面對旅遊通膨、過度觀光、以及被過度包裝內容掏空的「真實體驗」這三股夾擊，貨架探索恰好做到了這件事。  
**一、把「省錢」重新定義為「可及」，而非「將就」。**全球旅遊愈來愈昂貴，產業的預設解方是販售高價的獨佔感。貨架探索反其道而行：一籃四歐元的地方零食，就能以品嚐套餐的零頭換得一次真實的文化接觸。這是對通膨的草根回應，讓學生、家庭與預算型旅客都能「有意義地旅行」，而非被高價擋在門外。  
**二、把觀光消費導回日常在地經濟。**傳統觀光把金錢集中在景點周邊少數商家，塞車與擁擠卻由居民承擔。當旅客改在居民採買的地方消費，錢便流向社區雜貨店、地方食品生產者與少見於旅遊書的小品牌。這是一種安靜的經濟去中心化，讓觀光紅利分配得更平均，也紓解了熱點的承載壓力。  
**三、以「日常」重建文化理解。**一間超市，是一個社會如何飲食、定價、包裝與生活的未經修飾索引。閱讀貨架——當季食材、份量習慣、什麼便宜什麼昂貴——所帶來的文化識讀，是任何佈景式景點都給不了的。在充斥合成與高度包裝內容的時代，「平凡」本身正是價值：它是「真實生活的證據」。  
**四、與永續相互呼應。**購買在地日常品，能減少對進口、過度包裝旅遊食品的依賴，並支持更短的供應鏈。貨架旅遊被形容為旅遊通膨的解藥，正因它同時對資源更為友善。

#### **可行的啟示與行動建議**

**對城市與觀光行銷組織：**不只推廣地標，更可發布「在地市場路線」，引導旅客走進社區雜貨店與傳統市場，把人潮從飽和景點分散到樂於接待的社區。並與里鄰、住戶組織合作，讓紅利被歡迎、而非被抗拒。  
**對小型商家與地方生產者：**加上簡單的多語貨架小卡（「推薦這款在地特產」）、接受常見的旅遊支付方式，並設計一份招牌商品的「旅人採買籃」。低成本的小改變，就能把好奇的旅客轉化為新的營收來源。  
**對社會企業與非營利組織：**善用此趨勢，把旅客消費導向合作社、由難民經營的食品事業或公平貿易在地品牌，讓一個爆紅習慣，變成具包容性的經濟參與。  
**對旅客：**把逛超市當成刻意的文化學習。盡量向獨立與合作社商店消費，向店員請教他們的心頭好，並分享你「學到什麼」，而不只是「買到什麼」。

#### **結語**

貨架探索提醒我們：有意義的創新，往往就藏在眼前。透過重新賦予最平凡空間新的價值，旅人們無意間找到了一套更便宜、更包容、更永續、也更文化誠實的模式——並悄悄地對它所身處的觀光產業提出了批判。對城市、商家與社會企業而言，機會就在於：認出「走道也是景點」，並刻意為它所能促成的公平價值分配，用心設計。

## **English Draft**

### **The Right to Fail Out Loud: Flop-Core and the Social Innovation of Honest Imperfection**

#### **Introduction**

This week's standout culture trend is a rebellion against a decade of curated perfection. Called "Flop-Core," it has creators posting their failures instead of their wins: the recipe that collapsed, the goal they missed, the event they never made it to. A typical post pairs a cheerful selfie with a caption like "it's August and I've been to 0 events"—the mismatch between the smile and the confession is the whole joke. Deliberately sharing failures under the hashtag \#flop has surged, and it fits a broader 2026 shift documented across social reports: as feeds flood with synthetic, AI-generated content, audiences pay a premium for "proof of humanity." Flop-Core looks like comedy, but underneath it is a genuine social innovation in how a culture handles failure, comparison and mental health.

#### **The Social Innovation Angle**

Social innovation includes new social norms and cultural practices that make wellbeing more attainable for more people. Flop-Core is a bottom-up norm change addressing a well-documented harm: the link between highlight-reel social media and rising anxiety, comparison and inadequacy, especially among young people.  
**1\. It decouples worth from performance.** The highlight reel taught a generation that only wins are shareable, quietly equating visible success with value. By making the flop postable, the trend widens the range of "acceptable" self-presentation. That is a norm shift with mental-health consequences: if failure is normal and even funny, the shame that isolates people loses its grip.  
**2\. It rebuilds trust through vulnerability.** A peer-reviewed 2026 study on influencers' proactive disclosure of failures found that admitting flops can enhance perceived authenticity. In an attention economy corroded by staged content and AI fakes, honest imperfection becomes a scarce, trusted signal. Flop-Core effectively crowdsources a new credibility currency—one harder for polished marketing to counterfeit.  
**3\. It is a distributed peer-support system in disguise.** When thousands publicly admit missed goals and small failures, they create a shared corrective to the illusion that everyone else is thriving. This is community mental-health infrastructure built not by institutions but by ordinary users—accessible, stigma-reducing and free.  
**4\. It carries real risks that responsible innovation must manage.** A trend can be co-opted: brands may perform "strategic flops" to appear relatable, and "failure" can itself become a new competitive aesthetic. If curated flops replace curated wins, the underlying pressure returns in disguise. The innovation is healthy only while it stays honest and non-commercialized.

#### **Actionable Implications**

**For educators and schools:** Borrow the format deliberately. "Flop of the week" reflections—where students share a mistake and what it taught them—normalize productive failure, a cornerstone of resilience and a growth mindset. The trend gives teachers a culturally fluent hook.  
**For employers and teams:** Institutionalize honest imperfection through blameless post-mortems and "failure résumés," where leaders share their own missteps first. Psychological safety—knowing you can admit a flop without punishment—is the single strongest predictor of high-performing teams.  
**For mental-health organizations:** Meet young people where the conversation already is. Partner with creators to pair the humor of Flop-Core with real coping resources, so the trend becomes a doorway to support rather than just a punchline.  
**For brands:** Resist the temptation to fake vulnerability. Audiences increasingly detect performative authenticity; the credible move is transparency about genuine setbacks, not manufactured relatability.

#### **Conclusion**

Flop-Core is more than a meme; it is a culture quietly rewriting its relationship with failure. By making imperfection shareable, ordinary users have built an accessible, stigma-reducing correction to a decade of comparison culture. The task now is stewardship: to protect the honesty at its core from commercial capture, and to translate a viral joke into durable practices—in classrooms, teams and support systems—that give people a lasting right to fail out loud.

### **大聲失敗的權利：Flop-Core 與「誠實不完美」的社會創新**

#### **引言**

本週最搶眼的文化趨勢，是一場對十年來「精修完美」的反叛。名為「Flop-Core（坦承失敗風潮）」，創作者們貼出的不再是勝利，而是失敗：塌掉的食譜、沒達成的目標、最後沒去成的活動。典型的貼文，會配上一張開心的自拍，加上「都八月了，我一場活動都沒去過」這類文案——笑點正在於笑容與坦白之間的落差。刻意在 \#flop 標籤下分享失敗的風氣急遽升溫，也呼應了 2026 年多份社群報告所記錄的大轉向：當動態被合成、AI 生成內容淹沒，觀眾願意為「人性的證據」付出溢價。Flop-Core 看似搞笑，底層卻是一場關於「一個文化如何面對失敗、比較與心理健康」的真實社會創新。

#### **社會創新角度分析**

社會創新也包含「讓更多人更容易獲得幸福感」的新社會規範與文化實踐。Flop-Core 正是一場由下而上的規範轉變，直指一項被大量研究證實的傷害：精修式社群與年輕人日益升高的焦慮、比較與自我不足感之間的關聯。  
**一、把「價值」與「表現」脫鉤。**精修動態教會了一整個世代：只有勝利值得分享，並悄悄把「看得見的成功」等同於「價值」。當失敗也能被貼出，這股風潮拓寬了「可被接受」的自我呈現範圍。這是一項具心理健康意涵的規範轉移：若失敗是常態、甚至好笑，那使人孤立的羞恥感便鬆動了。  
**二、以脆弱重建信任。**2026 年一份針對網紅「主動揭露失敗」的同儕審查研究發現，坦承失敗能提升被感知的真實性。在一個被佈景內容與 AI 造假侵蝕的注意力經濟中，誠實的不完美成了稀缺而可信的訊號。Flop-Core 實際上是群眾外包出一種新的可信度貨幣——一種讓精緻行銷更難偽造的貨幣。  
**三、它是一套偽裝過的分散式同儕支持系統。**當成千上萬人公開承認未達成的目標與小小的失敗，他們共同修正了「別人都過得很好」的錯覺。這是一種社區心理健康的基礎設施，不是由機構、而是由一般使用者所建立——可及、去汙名、且免費。  
**四、它也帶有必須被負責任地管理的風險。**趨勢可能被收編：品牌可能演出「策略性失敗」以顯得平易近人，「失敗」本身也可能變成新的競爭美學。若「精修的失敗」取代了「精修的勝利」，底層的壓力只是換了張臉重返。唯有維持誠實、不被商業化，這項創新才是健康的。

#### **可行的啟示與行動建議**

**對教育者與學校：**刻意借用這個形式。「本週失敗」的反思——讓學生分享一個錯誤及它帶來的學習——能把「有建設性的失敗」正常化，而這正是韌性與成長型思維的基石。這股趨勢給了老師一個貼近學生語言的切入點。  
**對雇主與團隊：**把「誠實的不完美」制度化，透過不究責的事後檢討與「失敗履歷」，並由主管率先分享自己的失誤。心理安全感——知道你可以承認失敗而不被懲罰——是高績效團隊最強的單一預測因子。  
**對心理健康組織：**到年輕人對話發生的地方去。與創作者合作，把 Flop-Core 的幽默與真實的因應資源結合，讓趨勢成為通往支持的門，而不只是一個笑點。  
**對品牌：**抗拒偽裝脆弱的誘惑。觀眾愈來愈能識破表演式的真實；可信的做法是對真正的挫折保持透明，而非製造出來的親和感。

#### **結語**

Flop-Core 不只是一個迷因，而是一個文化正悄悄改寫它與失敗的關係。透過讓不完美變得可分享，一般使用者建起了一套可及、去汙名的修正機制，回應十年來的比較文化。如今的課題是「守護」：保護其核心的誠實不被商業收編，並把一個爆紅的玩笑，轉化為課堂、團隊與支持系統中能長久留存的實踐——讓人們擁有一份持久的、「大聲失敗」的權利。

### **Open-Source Self-Improvement: What "10/10 Habits" Teaches Us About Democratizing Personal Growth**

#### **Introduction**

Alongside the week's lighter trends sits a striking self-improvement wave: "10/10 habits." Using the format "10/10 habits to…," Gen Z creators share specific, achievable routines meant to help followers reach their potential—dressing "like the millionaire version of yourself" for a week, staying off your phone for the first 30–60 minutes after waking, or "10/10 hobbies to become disgustingly intelligent." The framing is deliberately concrete and actionable, trading vague motivation for a checklist you can start today. Beneath the productivity gloss, the trend is a live experiment in how personal development gets distributed, and by whom—making it fertile ground for a social innovation reading.

#### **The Social Innovation Angle**

Personal development has long been a paid, gatekept market: books, courses, coaches and retreats priced out of reach for many. "10/10 habits" represents a peer-to-peer, open-source alternative—and that shift is the innovation.  
**1\. It democratizes access to self-improvement knowledge.** What was once locked inside expensive coaching is now free, bite-sized and searchable. A teenager with no budget can access the same practical habit stacks as someone paying for a life coach. This lowers the barrier to "cultural capital"—the routines, framings and disciplines that quietly compound into opportunity—and can help narrow, rather than widen, existing gaps.  
**2\. It reformats knowledge for how people actually learn now.** By packaging growth as specific, small, immediately doable actions ("phone-free mornings"), the trend uses behavioral-design principles—tiny habits, clear cues—far more effectively than a 300-page book most people never finish. The format itself is a social technology for behavior change.  
**3\. It builds accountability and community.** Because creators and commenters share progress, the trend layers social accountability onto private goals. Peer visibility—people cheering, comparing notes, returning with updates—is a documented driver of habit adherence, and here it emerges organically rather than being sold.  
**4\. It carries real risks worth naming.** Unvetted advice can be shallow, contradictory or subtly harmful; "become disgustingly intelligent" can tip into toxic hustle culture, over-optimization and burnout. The same democratization that removes gatekeepers also removes quality control. Responsible innovation here means pairing accessibility with credibility and self-compassion.

#### **Actionable Implications**

**For educators and youth programs:** Meet the appetite, upgrade the substance. Co-create "10/10 habits" content with credentialed input—study skills, sleep, focus—so the format's reach carries evidence-based advice. Teach media literacy alongside it: how to evaluate whether a viral habit is actually supported.  
**For social enterprises and NGOs:** The format is a low-cost distribution channel for genuinely useful life skills—financial basics, mental-health hygiene, civic participation—to audiences that formal programs struggle to reach. Package impact knowledge the way the algorithm rewards.  
**For public-health and wellbeing bodies:** Redirect the "extreme discipline" energy toward sustainable wellbeing. Promote habit content that emphasizes rest, balance and mental health, offering a healthier counter-narrative to grind culture within the same trend.  
**For individuals:** Treat the feed as a menu, not a mandate. Adopt one or two habits that fit your life, verify anything consequential, and measure success by consistency and wellbeing—not by out-optimizing strangers online.

#### **Conclusion**

"10/10 habits" is more than motivational content; it is a glimpse of self-improvement becoming open-source—free, peer-driven and formatted for real behavior change. That democratization is a genuine social good, expanding who gets access to the quiet disciplines that shape opportunity. The work ahead is to raise the signal: to pair the format's reach and accessibility with credibility, balance and self-compassion, so that a viral checklist becomes not a new source of pressure, but a shared, equitable on-ramp to growth.
