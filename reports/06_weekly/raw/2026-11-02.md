Title: When Walls Learn to Breathe — What Moss-Growing Concrete Teaches Us About Social Innovation

Introduction  
This week a quietly radical idea went viral: a Dutch startup, Respyre, has engineered concrete that grows its own moss. Spun out of research at TU Delft, the material — variously called Mosscrete or VertiScape — is "bioreceptive." Its porosity, mineral content and pH are tuned so that a sprayed-on bio-gel of moss spores takes hold on bare walls, typically establishing a living green surface within about twelve weeks. Much of the substrate is made from recycled rubble and reactivated cement, and the company already reports a handful of real deployments, including a test beside Amsterdam's NEMO Science Museum and on graffiti-prone bridge piers. In a week dominated by elections and AI headlines, the image of a grey wall slowly turning green struck a nerve. The reason it resonates is not the novelty of plants on buildings — vertical gardens are old news — but the way it reframes a hostile surface as a host.

The Social Innovation Lens  
Social innovation is less about a single clever product than about shifting the relationship between a problem, the people it affects, and the systems that sustain it. Moss-growing concrete is interesting precisely because it attacks several entrenched systems at once, and it does so by addition rather than demolition.

First, it is a retrofit logic rather than a rebuild logic. The great unspoken cost of "green cities" is that most of our cities already exist. We cannot bulldoze the grey infrastructure of the twentieth century and start again; the carbon and capital locked into existing concrete is enormous. Respyre's proposition — apply a bioreceptive layer to surfaces that are already there — treats the existing city as the substrate for renewal. This is a hallmark of mature social innovation: working with the stock you have, not the utopia you wish you had.

Second, it democratizes greening. Conventional living walls are expensive, irrigation-hungry and maintenance-intensive — effectively luxury goods for corporate lobbies and premium developments. Moss, by contrast, needs no soil, draws moisture largely from the air and rain, and after an establishment period requires little intervention. If the economics hold, greening stops being a signal of wealth and becomes a baseline standard available to social housing, transit tunnels, underpasses and school walls. The social-equity dimension — who gets to live near green surfaces, who breathes cooler, cleaner air — is where this moves from "green tech" to social innovation.

Third, it bundles co-benefits that usually require separate budgets. A single moss wall can lower surface temperature and soften the urban heat island, capture particulate matter, dampen noise, support micro-biodiversity, and deter graffiti. In public-sector terms, that is an environmental, a health and a maintenance line item solved by one intervention. Innovations that collapse several siloed budgets into one tend to scale, because they give different stakeholders their own reason to say yes.

There are honest caveats. Independent verification of the cooling and air-quality claims is thin; the company is still raising a seed round; and moss performs differently across climates and pollution loads. A humid Dutch façade is not a dry subtropical one. Social innovation is damaged, not helped, by overclaiming — and the hype cycle around "miracle materials" has burned public trust before.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
1\. For cities and public agencies: pilot on the ugliest, most neglected surfaces first — retaining walls, flyover columns, tunnel mouths. These are low-risk, high-visibility sites where even a partial success builds political permission for wider rollout. Write co-benefits (heat, air, noise) into the procurement scoring so the bid is not judged on upfront cost alone.  
2\. For developers and landlords: treat bioreceptive surfaces as a maintenance-reduction and tenant-wellbeing investment, not a marketing veneer. Measure and publish before/after surface temperatures so the claim is accountable.  
3\. For social entrepreneurs: the transferable idea is "bioreceptivity" — designing the built environment to invite life rather than repel it. Ask where else a hostile surface could be re-engineered as a host: rooftops, seawalls, noise barriers, even plastic waste.  
4\. For citizens and communities: push for greening to be measured by distribution, not by flagship projects. One spectacular living wall downtown matters less than a hundred ordinary green walls in the neighborhoods that are hottest and poorest.

The deeper lesson of this week's viral wall is a mindset: innovation that heals the city need not start from scratch. Sometimes it starts by teaching what we already have to breathe.

標題：當牆壁學會呼吸——會長苔蘚的混凝土，給社會創新的一堂課

引言  
本週一則看似溫和、其實激進的消息在網路上發酵：荷蘭新創公司 Respyre 研發出「會自己長苔蘚」的混凝土。這項源自台夫特理工大學（TU Delft）研究、被稱為 Mosscrete 或 VertiScape 的材料，具有「生物可附著性」（bioreceptive）——它的孔隙率、礦物成分與酸鹼值經過調校，讓噴塗上去的苔蘚孢子生物凝膠能在光禿的牆面存活，通常約十二週就能形成一片活的綠色表面。基材有相當比例來自回收碎料與再活化水泥，公司也已累積數個實際案例，包括阿姆斯特丹 NEMO 科學博物館旁的試驗，以及容易被塗鴉的橋墩。在一個被選舉與 AI 新聞佔據的星期，一面灰牆緩緩轉綠的畫面格外觸動人心。它之所以引起共鳴，並不是因為「在建築上種植物」有多新奇——垂直花園早已不稀奇——而在於它把一個「敵意表面」重新定義為「宿主」。

社會創新角度分析  
社會創新的重點，從來不只是某個聰明的產品，而是改變「問題、受影響的人、以及維繫這個問題的系統」三者之間的關係。會長苔蘚的混凝土之所以值得談，正是因為它同時鬆動了好幾個根深柢固的系統，而且是用「加法」而非「拆除」的方式。

第一，它是「改造」的邏輯，而非「重建」的邏輯。綠色城市最少被說出口的成本是：我們的城市大多早就存在了。我們無法把二十世紀留下的灰色基礎設施全部推倒重來，既有混凝土所鎖住的碳與資本極其龐大。Respyre 的主張——在既有表面上塗覆一層生物可附著層——等於把現有城市當成更新的基底。這正是成熟社會創新的特徵：善用你手上的存量，而不是你想像中的烏托邦。

第二，它讓「綠化」變得更平等。傳統的生物牆造價高昂、耗水、維護繁瑣，實際上是企業大廳與高級建案的奢侈品。相較之下，苔蘚不需土壤、主要從空氣與雨水取得水分，度過建立期後幾乎不需人為介入。如果經濟效益成立，綠化就不再是財富的象徵，而能成為社會住宅、捷運隧道、地下道與校園牆面都負擔得起的基本配備。「誰能住在綠色表面附近、誰能呼吸到更涼爽潔淨的空氣」這個社會公平面向，正是它從「綠色科技」升級為「社會創新」的關鍵。

第三，它把原本需要分開編列的效益「打包」在一起。一面苔蘚牆可以同時降低表面溫度、緩解都市熱島、捕捉懸浮微粒、吸收噪音、支持微型生物多樣性，還能嚇阻塗鴉。對公部門而言，這是用一項措施同時處理了環境、健康與維護三條預算。能把多個各自為政的預算合而為一的創新，往往更容易規模化，因為它給了不同利害關係人各自點頭的理由。

當然也有必須誠實面對的保留。關於降溫與空氣品質的獨立驗證仍然薄弱；公司還在募集種子輪資金；苔蘚在不同氣候與污染負荷下的表現差異很大——潮濕的荷蘭立面，和乾燥的副熱帶牆面不是同一回事。社會創新最怕的就是誇大其詞，「神奇材料」的炒作週期過去已多次消耗公眾信任。

可行的啟示與行動建議  
1\. 對城市與公部門：先從最醜、最被忽視的表面下手——擋土牆、高架橋柱、隧道口。這些是低風險、高能見度的場域，即使只是局部成功，也能累積推動更大規模的政治許可。並把共同效益（降溫、空氣、噪音）寫進採購評分，讓標案不只比拚初期造價。  
2\. 對開發商與房東：把生物可附著表面視為「降低維護成本、提升住戶福祉」的投資，而非行銷門面。測量並公開施作前後的表面溫度，讓宣稱可被究責。  
3\. 對社會創業者：可移轉的概念是「生物可附著性」——把建成環境設計成「邀請生命」而非「排斥生命」。想想還有哪裡能把敵意表面重新改造成宿主：屋頂、海堤、隔音牆，甚至塑膠廢料。  
4\. 對市民與社區：要求以「分布」而非「旗艦工程」來衡量綠化。市中心一面壯觀的生物牆，遠不如最炎熱、最貧窮街區裡的一百面普通綠牆來得重要。

本週這面爆紅的牆，更深的啟示是一種心態：能療癒城市的創新，不一定要從零開始。有時候，它始於教會我們「已經擁有的東西」如何呼吸。

Title: Can You Redevelop a Neighborhood Without Evicting Its Flavor? The Nanjichang Lesson

Introduction  
This week Taipei signed off on a long-awaited public urban-renewal deal for "Unit 4" of the Nanjichang integrated-housing complex — one of the city's oldest social-housing blocks, damaged and yellow-tagged after the 2024 Hualien earthquake. The plan: two 24-storey residential towers, with ground and second floors kept as shops, upper floors providing around 285 regular homes plus 17 "happiness housing" (affordable) units. Resident participation reportedly reached 92.82%. But the headline that travelled furthest was not the financing (an estimated NT\$3.3–3.9 billion) — it was a quote from the developer promising to "keep talking to local vendors so the night market never stops operating," and a stall owner's plain reply: "We don't want to leave the night market." Nanjichang Night Market is a legendary cheap-eats institution. The anxiety underneath the week's coverage is universal: when the building is renewed, does the flavor survive?

The Social Innovation Lens  
Nanjichang is a near-perfect case study in a question social innovators keep running into: how do you upgrade the physical fabric of a place without destroying the invisible economy and community that made it worth saving?

Start with what a night market actually is. It is not a collection of real estate; it is a dense, informal economy of micro-enterprises, many of them decades old, operating on thin margins, trust, and foot traffic. Its value is relational — regular customers, supplier relationships, recipes, a specific rhythm of the street. None of that appears on a land-value appraisal. Standard redevelopment accounting sees square meters and sale prices (here, a projected NT\$7.6 billion in total sales); it is structurally blind to social capital. The social-innovation task is to make the invisible visible and fundable.

Three design choices in this deal deserve scrutiny, because they are where social innovation either happens or fails.

First, the 92.82% consent rate. High participation is genuinely progressive — Taiwan's public urban-renewal model (the Urban Redevelopment Center) at least forces negotiation rather than simple expropriation. But consent among resident-owners is not the same as protection for vendors and tenants, who are often the economically weakest and least represented in the room. Social innovation asks: whose vote counted, and whose livelihood is merely "consulted"?

Second, the decision to keep floors one and two as shops. This is the hinge. Physically preserving retail space is necessary but not sufficient. The real risk is the "renovation premium" — new shopfronts command new rents, and the NT\$50-per-bowl economy that defines the market cannot survive a glossy mall-style rent. Many cities have lovingly rebuilt a market only to price out the very vendors who were its soul, replacing them with chain tenants. Preserving the flavor requires preserving the price structure, not just the floor plan.

Third, the "happiness housing" allocation (17 units). Mixed-income provisioning is a sound principle, but 17 affordable units against 285 market homes signals that redevelopment economics still lean heavily on market sales to pencil out. That is the honest tension of public-private renewal everywhere: the market cross-subsidy that makes the project viable is the same force that gentrifies it.

The genuinely innovative path here is not to stop redevelopment — the building was earthquake-damaged; safety is non-negotiable. It is to treat the vendor economy as protected infrastructure, with the same seriousness as the structural engineering.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
1\. Guarantee a "right of return" with capped rents. Give existing vendors a contractual option to return to the rebuilt market at affordability-linked rents for a defined period, with transparent, phased increases. This single mechanism is the difference between preservation and polite eviction.  
2\. Manage the transition, not just the endpoint. The years of construction are where informal economies die — customers form new habits elsewhere. Fund a temporary relocation market nearby and actively market it, so foot traffic and vendor income survive the build.  
3\. Create a vendor seat at the table with real power. Consultation is not co-design. A vendors' cooperative with a formal role in leasing decisions turns "we don't want to leave" from a lament into leverage.  
4\. Measure success by retention, not by sales. Publish, five years on, how many of the original stalls are still trading. That number — not the NT\$7.6 billion sales figure — is the true test of whether this was redevelopment or replacement.

Nanjichang's deeper lesson: a neighborhood's worth is carried by its people and their trades, not only its walls. Renew the building; refuse to renew away the flavor.

標題：能不「趕走味道」地更新一個社區嗎？——南機場給我們的一課

引言  
本週台北市拍板了等待多年的南機場整宅「單元四」公辦都更案。這裡是全市最老的整建住宅之一，在 2024 年花蓮地震後受損、被列為黃單建築。計畫內容是：興建兩棟地上 24 層的住宅大樓，一、二樓保留為店舖，樓上提供約 285 戶一般住宅與 17 戶「幸福住宅」（平價住宅），住戶參與同意率據報達 92.82%。但傳得最遠的標題，不是估計 33 至 39 億元的資金，而是實施者承諾會「持續與在地店家溝通，確保夜市運作不中斷」，以及一位攤商樸素的回應：「不想離開夜市。」南機場夜市是傳奇般的平價美食重鎮。本週報導底下的焦慮其實是普世的：當房子更新了，味道還會在嗎？

社會創新角度分析  
南機場幾乎是社會創新者反覆遇到的一道題目的完美案例：你要如何更新一個地方的硬體，卻又不摧毀那個讓它值得被保留的無形經濟與社群？

先想清楚夜市究竟是什麼。它不是一堆房地產，而是一個密集的、非正式的微型企業經濟體——其中許多攤商經營數十年，靠著微薄利潤、信任與人流存活。它的價值是「關係性」的——熟客、供應商關係、配方、街道特有的節奏。這些都不會出現在土地估價表上。標準的都更會計看到的是坪數與售價（此案預估總銷約 76 億元），它在結構上對「社會資本」視而不見。社會創新的任務，就是把無形的東西變得可見、可被投資。

這個案子裡有三個設計抉擇值得細看，因為社會創新成敗往往就在這裡。

第一，92.82% 的同意率。高參與度確實是進步的——台灣的公辦都更模式（住都中心）至少迫使各方協商，而非單純徵收。但「住戶（所有權人）同意」不等於「攤商與承租戶受到保護」，後者往往是經濟上最弱勢、在談判桌上最缺席的一群。社會創新要問的是：誰的票算數，誰的生計只是被「諮詢」？

第二，把一、二樓保留為店舖的決定。這是關鍵樞紐。實體保留零售空間是必要的，但並不充分。真正的風險是「裝修溢價」——嶄新的店面會帶來新的租金，而定義這個夜市的「一碗五十元」經濟，撐不起百貨商場式的光鮮租金。許多城市曾用心重建一座市場，結果卻把它的靈魂——那些攤商——逼到付不起租金而離開，換上了連鎖品牌。要保留味道，就得保留價格結構，而不只是樓層平面圖。

第三，「幸福住宅」的配額（17 戶）。混合所得的供給是個好原則，但 17 戶平價住宅對上 285 戶市場住宅，說明都更的財務邏輯仍高度依賴市場銷售才能打平。這正是世界各地公私協力都更誠實的張力：讓專案可行的市場交叉補貼，同時也是讓它仕紳化的那股力量。

這裡真正創新的路徑，不是阻止都更——房子有震損，安全不容妥協——而是把攤商經濟當成「受保護的基礎設施」，用對待結構工程同樣的嚴肅態度來對待它。

可行的啟示與行動建議  
1\. 以「租金上限」保障「回遷權」。給既有攤商一份契約選擇權，可在一定年限內以與可負擔性連動的租金回到重建後的市場，並採透明、分階段的調漲。光是這一個機制，就是「保存」與「客氣地驅離」之間的分水嶺。  
2\. 管理「過渡期」，而不只是「終點」。施工的那幾年，正是非正式經濟死亡的時刻——顧客會在別處養成新習慣。應在附近設置臨時安置市場並積極行銷，讓人流與攤商收入撐過工程期。  
3\. 給攤商一個「有實權」的席位。諮詢不等於共同設計。一個在租賃決策上有正式角色的攤商合作社，能把「不想離開」從哀嘆變成籌碼。  
4\. 以「留存率」而非「銷售額」衡量成功。五年後，請公開原有攤商還有多少仍在營業。那個數字——而不是 76 億元的銷售數字——才是檢驗這究竟是「更新」還是「替換」的真正標準。

南機場更深的一課：一個社區的價值，由它的人與他們的生意承載，而不只是它的牆。更新那棟房子吧；但拒絕把味道一起更新掉。

Title: The Algorithm Discovered a Tea Town — Now Comes the Hard Part

Introduction  
This week a single travel post did what decades of tourism brochures could not: it made New Taipei's Pinglin Old Street go viral. A visitor described arriving around 9am to near-empty streets, only to watch crowds pour in by the afternoon; the post drew roughly 14,000 likes and sent a wave of day-trippers up the mountain road. Pinglin is a small tea town, its roughly 200-meter old street lined with shops that weave the local "Baozhong" tea into everything — tea rice cakes, tea mantou, tea nougat, tea-oil noodles, tea ice cream, tea mochi. Visitors praised the slow pace, "perfect for travelers who don't want to rush." It is a charming, feel-good story. It is also a stress test — because being discovered by the algorithm is not the same as being ready for it.

The Social Innovation Lens  
Pinglin sits at the center of what in Taiwan is called 地方創生 (regional revitalization) and globally goes by place-based development: the effort to keep aging rural towns economically alive and demographically young without turning them into theme parks. This week's virality makes the stakes vivid.

The opportunity is real. For small towns, demand is the scarcest resource. Young people leave because there is no living to be made; businesses close because there are not enough customers; the loss compounds. A viral post injects exactly what is missing — attention, foot traffic, cash. And crucially, Pinglin already did the hard upstream work: it built a distinctive, place-rooted product. Its tea is not a generic souvenir but a local asset turned into a hundred edible variations. That is textbook asset-based community development — building on what a place uniquely has rather than importing a formula. The virality worked because the substance was already there.

But social innovation is interested in what happens after the spike, because the spike is where most revitalization efforts quietly fail in one of two directions.

The first failure is the boom-bust whiplash. Algorithmic attention is volatile and undiscerning. A town that reorganizes itself around a viral moment — hiring, stocking, raising rents in expectation of permanent crowds — is dangerously exposed when the algorithm moves on next month. Resilient revitalization treats a viral spike as volatile working capital, not as a new baseline.

The second failure is over-tourism eating the very thing that drew people. The quality visitors praised — the slow pace, the uncrowded authenticity — is precisely what weekend crowds destroy. Pinglin also sits in a protected water-source reserve supplying greater Taipei, so carrying capacity is not a slogan; it is hydrology. If the town trades its watershed and its calm for a few viral weekends, it will have sold its only durable advantage for a short-term gain.

The deeper innovation question is one of ownership and distribution. Who captures the value of this attention? If it flows to a few photogenic storefronts and absentee landlords, the viral moment accelerates inequality within the town. If it is channeled through tea cooperatives, shared branding, and reinvestment in local infrastructure, it compounds into genuine community wealth. The algorithm is neutral; the institutions around it are not.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
1\. Convert the spike into a membership, not a one-night stand. Capture visiting day-trippers into a relationship — a tea-club subscription, an "adopt-a-tea-terrace" scheme, mail-order with the maker's story. One repeat customer is worth more than ten algorithmic strangers.  
2\. Spread load across time and space. Actively market weekday and off-season visits, and design routes that push visitors beyond the 200-meter bottleneck toward the Tea Museum, riverside trails and outlying farms — protecting both the experience and the watershed.  
3\. Pool the gains. A town-level cooperative or shared "Pinglin tea" brand lets small makers negotiate, co-market and reinvest together, so the value of virality accrues to the community rather than to whoever happened to be most photogenic that week.  
4\. Protect the asset that made it special. Set and communicate a visitor carrying capacity tied to the water reserve. Paradoxically, visible limits ("book ahead, we cap numbers") enhance the slow, exclusive appeal that went viral in the first place.

Pinglin's lesson for every overlooked town: the algorithm can hand you a crowd, but only you can decide whether that crowd becomes a community or a stampede. Revitalization is not the viral moment — it is everything you build to outlast it.

標題：演算法發現了一座茶鄉——真正的考驗現在才開始

引言  
本週，一篇旅遊貼文做到了數十年觀光宣傳做不到的事：讓新北坪林老街爆紅。一位遊客描述自己上午九點多抵達時街道還很冷清，到了午後卻湧入大量人潮；貼文獲得約 1.4 萬人按讚，把一波又一波的一日遊旅客送上山路。坪林是座小小的茶鄉，約 200 公尺長的老街上，店家把在地「包種茶」融進各種料理——茶粿、茶饅頭、茶牛軋糖、茶油麵線、茶冰淇淋、茶麻糬。遊客稱讚這裡步調慢，「很適合不趕行程的旅客」。這是個迷人、療癒的故事，但它同時也是一場壓力測試——因為「被演算法發現」，和「準備好被發現」，是兩回事。

社會創新角度分析  
坪林正好位於台灣所謂「地方創生」、國際上稱為「地方本位發展」（place-based development）的核心課題上：如何在不把鄉鎮變成主題樂園的前提下，讓逐漸老化的鄉村在經濟上存活、在人口上回春。本週的爆紅，把這場賭注變得格外鮮明。

機會是真實的。對小鎮而言，最稀缺的資源就是「需求」。年輕人離開，因為無以為生；店家歇業，因為客人不夠；流失會不斷複利。一篇爆紅貼文，注入的正是最缺的東西——關注、人流、現金。而且關鍵在於，坪林早已把上游的苦工做完了：它打造出一個獨特、扎根於地方的產品。它的茶不是通用的紀念品，而是把在地資產轉化成上百種可食用的變化。這正是教科書般的「資產導向社區發展」（asset-based community development）——建立在一個地方獨有的東西上，而不是移植一套公式。爆紅之所以奏效，是因為底蘊早就在那裡。

但社會創新真正關心的，是「尖峰之後」會發生什麼，因為絕大多數地方創生的努力，正是在尖峰過後悄悄地從兩個方向之一失敗。

第一種失敗，是「暴起暴落」的甩尾。演算法給的關注既善變又不挑。一個圍繞爆紅瞬間重新組織自己的小鎮——為了預期中的永久人潮而增聘人手、大量備貨、調漲租金——當演算法下個月轉移目標時，就會陷入危險的裸露部位。有韌性的地方創生，會把爆紅視為「波動的營運資金」，而不是「新的基準線」。

第二種失敗，是「過度觀光」反噬了當初吸引人的那個東西。遊客稱讚的特質——慢步調、不擁擠的純樸——正是週末人潮會摧毀的東西。坪林還位於供應大台北的水源保護區內，因此「承載量」不是口號，而是水文學。如果小鎮用它的集水區與寧靜，去換幾個爆紅的週末，那它等於為短期利益賣掉了自己唯一能持久的優勢。

更深層的創新課題，關乎「所有權」與「分配」。這波關注的價值被誰拿走？如果它只流向少數上相的店面與不在地的房東，爆紅瞬間反而會加速鎮內的不平等。如果它能透過茶農合作社、共同品牌、以及對在地基礎設施的再投資被導流，它就會複利成真正的社區財富。演算法是中立的；但圍繞它的制度不是。

可行的啟示與行動建議  
1\. 把尖峰轉化為「會員關係」，而非「一夜情」。把來訪的一日遊旅客轉化成一段關係——茶俱樂部訂閱、「認養一畦茶園」方案、附上製茶人故事的宅配。一個回頭客，勝過十個演算法送來的陌生人。  
2\. 在時間與空間上分散負荷。積極行銷平日與淡季造訪，並設計能把遊客從 200 公尺的瓶頸推向茶業博物館、溪畔步道與外圍茶園的路線——同時保護體驗品質與集水區。  
3\. 把收益「匯聚」起來。一個鎮級的合作社或共同的「坪林茶」品牌，能讓小農與小店一起議價、共同行銷、共同再投資，讓爆紅的價值歸於整個社區，而不是歸於那週剛好最上相的某一家。  
4\. 保護那個讓它特別的資產。設定並對外溝通與水源保護區連動的「遊客承載量」。弔詭的是，可見的限制（「請先預約，我們管制人數」）反而會強化當初爆紅的那種慢活、限定的吸引力。

坪林給每一個被忽略小鎮的一課：演算法能遞給你一群人，但只有你能決定，這群人會變成一個社區，還是一場踩踏。地方創生不是那個爆紅的瞬間——而是你為了「撐過它」所建立的一切。
