Title: When Saving Food Becomes a Design Problem: Social Innovation and the "Surplus App Mismatch"

Introduction  
This week a quietly important food story spread across sustainability and consumer circles: surplus-food apps—the services that connect restaurants, bakeries and supermarkets with shoppers willing to buy unsold items at a discount—are struggling to match meals with the people who want them. A late-October report noted that users abandon these apps when the merchant is too far away, the discount is too shallow, or the pickup window is inconvenient. The context is staggering: ReFED's 2026 U.S. Food Waste Report estimates roughly 70 million tons of food go unsold or uneaten in the United States each year, while Singapore alone generated about 790,000 tonnes of food waste in 2025\. At the same time, food-rescue organizations in North America are racing to scale—Minnesota's Second Harvest Heartland recovered more than 25,000 pounds of leftover State Fair food, and Canadian groups are building processing facilities to turn surplus into prepared meals. The headline is not "technology failed." It is that saving food is fundamentally a social and logistical design problem, not just a software one.

The Social Innovation Angle  
Social innovation asks a sharper question than "how do we sell leftovers?" It asks: how do we redesign the system so that surplus flows reliably to where it creates the most value—human, environmental and economic—at once? Three features of the current moment make it a genuine social-innovation frontier.  
1\. The mismatch is a symptom of thin "social infrastructure," not thin technology. Apps like Too Good To Go proved demand exists, but a transaction app treats surplus as a retail problem. The deeper issue is coordination: a bakery's surplus appears unpredictably at 8 p.m., while the household that needs it most may lack transport, time, or a smartphone. A University of Houston study this month found that higher-income consumers actually valued surplus-food discount information more than lower-income consumers—a warning that market mechanisms alone can route "rescued" food toward the already-comfortable, not toward food insecurity. Social innovation closes this gap by adding intermediaries, trust and shared logistics that pure markets omit.  
2\. Food rescue is professionalizing—and that is the real innovation. The shift from ad-hoc charity pickups to dedicated processing facilities (chopping, cooking and freezing surplus produce before it spoils) is a structural leap. It converts a volatile, perishable stream into stable, distributable meals—exactly the reliability that apps cannot guarantee. This is "social R\&D": non-profits are building the cold-chain and manufacturing capacity that the market under-provides because the margins are thin and the beneficiaries are poor.  
3\. The winning models are hybrids. The most promising designs blend a commercial app (to capture willing-to-pay demand and fund operations) with a civic layer (food banks, schools, community fridges) that absorbs the surplus markets won't touch. Value is measured not only in revenue but in meals delivered, carbon avoided and dignity preserved—a classic triple-bottom-line reframing.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
• For cities and governments: Treat surplus food as public infrastructure. Fund shared cold-storage and micro-processing hubs the way you fund recycling, and use tax incentives (Good Samaritan–style liability protection plus donation credits) to make giving cheaper than dumping.  
• For app builders: Design for the "last mile of dignity." Add scheduled pickups, volunteer-driver matching, and a toggle that lets merchants donate (not just discount) unsold batches to a verified community partner. Measure success in redistribution rate, not just gross merchandise value.  
• For food businesses: Forecast and pre-commit surplus. A bakery that signals likely surplus at noon—rather than at closing—gives rescue logistics time to work. Small predictability beats large good intentions.  
• For citizens and funders: Support community fridges, "mystery bag" programs and local food-rescue non-profits; they are the civic layer that makes the technology meaningful. Volunteer an hour of transport, and you fix the exact bottleneck the apps cannot.

The lesson of this week is hopeful: we do not lack surplus, demand, or even apps. We lack the connective tissue between them. That tissue—trust, logistics, shared infrastructure—is precisely what social innovation is built to grow.

社會創新問的問題比「如何賣掉剩菜」更鋒利：我們能否重新設計整個系統，讓剩食穩定地流向能同時創造最大人道、環境與經濟價值之處？當前情勢有三個特徵，使它成為真正的社會創新前沿。

三、勝出的模式是「混合型」。最有前景的設計，結合商業 App（擷取願付費需求、支撐營運）與公民層（食物銀行、學校、社區冰箱），由後者吸收市場不願碰的剩食。其價值衡量不只看營收，更看送出的餐數、減少的碳排與維護的尊嚴——這是典型的「三重底線」重構。

• 對城市與政府：把剩食視為公共基礎設施。像補助回收一樣補助共享冷儲與微型加工據點，並用稅務誘因（仿「好撒馬利亞人」免責條款＋捐贈抵稅）讓「捐出去」比「丟掉」更便宜。  
• 對 App 開發者：為「尊嚴的最後一哩」而設計。加入預約取貨、志工司機媒合，以及讓商家可「捐贈」（而非僅折價）給認證社區夥伴的選項。成功指標應是「重分配率」，而非僅看交易總額。  
• 對餐飲業者：預測並預先承諾剩食。在中午（而非打烊時）就釋出可能剩食訊號，能給救援物流足夠運作時間。小小的「可預測性」勝過龐大的善意。  
• 對公民與資助者：支持社區冰箱、「驚喜袋」計畫與在地食物救援組織；它們是讓科技產生意義的公民層。你捐出一小時的運輸，就能修補 App 無法解決的瓶頸。

本週的啟示是充滿希望的：我們並不缺剩食、缺需求、甚至不缺 App，我們缺的是它們之間的「連結組織」。而信任、物流與共享基礎設施，正是社會創新最擅長培育的東西。

Title: Co-Living Grows Up: How Shared Housing Became a Social-Innovation Answer to the Affordability Crisis

Introduction  
On October 28, housing-policy watchers noted a telling shift: co-living is "gaining planning support as councils look to widen housing choice." New research by planning consultancy Lichfields found that adopted policies and guidance now address co-living in just 7% of the 84 local authorities reviewed—small, but a clear signal that shared living is moving from the fringes into the planning mainstream, and increasingly outside London. The momentum is transatlantic: in the United States, Rhode Island lawmakers introduced the "ROOM Act" to strip away zoning, building-code and occupancy barriers to co-living and single-room-occupancy (SRO) homes, while co-living is quietly rising among older adults priced out of conventional housing. After a decade in which "co-living" often meant glossy, overpriced micro-studios for young professionals, the concept is being rediscovered as something more fundamental: a social innovation for sharing scarce, expensive space.

The Social Innovation Angle  
Housing is where the affordability crisis, loneliness epidemic and climate pressures collide—and co-living, properly designed, addresses all three at once. That multi-problem leverage is the hallmark of social innovation.  
1\. It reframes the unit of housing from "the private dwelling" to "the shared community." Twentieth-century housing policy optimized for self-contained family units—one kitchen, one living room, one household per door. But household sizes have shrunk, single-person living has surged, and that model now wastes space and money. Co-living's innovation is to privatize only the bedroom/bathroom and socialize the kitchen, lounge, workspace and laundry. The result can be dramatically cheaper rents and a built-in antidote to isolation—especially for older adults and young renters alike.  
2\. The real breakthrough is regulatory and civic, not architectural. Shared houses are nothing new; what's new is legalizing and standardizing them at scale. Many cities cap how many unrelated adults may share a dwelling, effectively freezing spare bedrooms out of the rental market. Rhode Island's ROOM Act and the UK councils' planning guidance are social innovations in the form of rules: they rewrite the operating system of housing so that sharing is permitted, safe and dignified rather than informal and precarious.  
3\. It demands a new "management" layer—community as a service. Co-living only works if someone curates compatibility, maintains shared spaces and seeds genuine community. Operators like PadSplit (affordable workforce housing) and Singapore's The Assembly Place show that the innovation is as much about social facilitation—house agreements, conflict resolution, events—as about bricks. This is where a cold "room rental" becomes a warm "chosen household."  
The risk, clearly, is that co-living becomes a loophole for cramming more people into less space at higher per-square-foot rents—gentrification with a beanbag. Social innovation succeeds only if affordability, minimum room standards and tenant protections are written into the policy from the start.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
• For policymakers: Codify co-living as its own use class with clear standards—minimum room sizes, shared-amenity ratios, fire safety and affordability quotas. Pair permission with protection so "widening choice" doesn't become "lowering standards."  
• For developers and operators: Compete on community, not just on filling beds. Invest in a resident-experience team, transparent house agreements, and designs that make sharing feel like a feature, not a compromise. Offer older-adult and intergenerational models, not only young-professional ones.  
• For social-housing providers and non-profits: Pilot co-living for groups the market ignores—seniors on fixed incomes, care leavers, people exiting homelessness. Shared living plus light-touch support can be both cheaper and more humane than isolated units.  
• For renters and communities: Treat co-living as a legitimate life stage, not a last resort. Advocate locally to relax outdated occupancy caps, which quietly keep millions of bedrooms empty while rents soar.

The deeper message: the housing crisis will not be solved by construction alone. Some of the most powerful "new housing" is the space we already have—unlocked by better rules, better management and a willingness to share.

標題：共居「轉正」——從英國規劃政策支持 co-living 看住房的社會創新

引言  
10 月 28 日，住房政策觀察者注意到一個耐人尋味的轉向：共居（co-living）正「隨著地方議會尋求拓寬住房選擇而獲得規劃政策支持」。規劃顧問公司 Lichfields 的新研究發現，在受檢視的 84 個地方政府中，已有 7% 在其採行的政策與指引中納入共居條款——比例雖小，卻清楚顯示「共享居住」正從邊緣走入規劃主流，而且愈來愈多發生在倫敦以外。這股動能橫跨大西洋：美國羅德島州議員提出「ROOM 法案」，意在拆除共居與單人房（SRO）住宅的土地分區、建築法規與入住人數障礙；同時，被傳統住房價格擠出的高齡族群，也悄悄轉向共居。在歷經十年、「共居」常指光鮮卻昂貴的年輕專業人士微型套房之後，這個概念正被重新發現為更根本的東西：一種「共享稀缺且昂貴空間」的社會創新。

社會創新角度分析  
住房是「可負擔性危機、孤獨流行病、氣候壓力」三者交會之處——而設計得當的共居，能一次回應這三者。這種「一石多鳥」的槓桿，正是社會創新的標誌。  
一、它把住房的基本單位，從「私人住宅」重構為「共享社群」。二十世紀的住房政策以自給自足的家庭單位為最佳化目標——一戶一廚、一廳、一門。但家戶規模已縮小、獨居暴增，這套模式如今既浪費空間也浪費金錢。共居的創新在於：只「私有化」臥室與衛浴，把廚房、客廳、工作區與洗衣間「社會化」。結果可能是租金大幅下降，以及一帖對抗孤立的內建解方——無論對高齡者或年輕租客皆然。  
二、真正的突破是在「法規與公民」層面，而非建築。合住的房子並不新鮮；新鮮的是「大規模地把它合法化、標準化」。許多城市限制多少無親屬關係的成人可共住一屋，等於把閒置臥室凍結在租賃市場之外。羅德島的 ROOM 法案與英國議會的規劃指引，是一種以「規則」為形式的社會創新：它們改寫住房的「作業系統」，讓「共享」變成被允許、安全且有尊嚴的選項，而非非正式且不穩定的灰色地帶。  
三、它需要一個全新的「管理」層——把社群當成一種服務。共居要運作，必須有人篩選相容性、維護共享空間、培育真正的社群。PadSplit（可負擔勞工住房）與新加坡 The Assembly Place 等營運者顯示：這項創新同樣關乎「社會促進」——住戶公約、衝突調解、社群活動——而不只是磚瓦。這正是讓冰冷的「出租房間」變成溫暖的「選擇而來的家」的關鍵。  
風險也很明顯：共居可能淪為「把更多人塞進更小空間、每平方英尺租金卻更高」的漏洞——換了懶骨頭沙發的仕紳化。社會創新唯有在政策一開始就把「可負擔性、最低房間標準與租客保障」寫入，才能成功。

可行啟示與行動建議  
• 對政策制定者：把共居訂為獨立的「使用類別」並設清楚標準——最低房間面積、共享設施比例、消防安全與可負擔比例。讓「許可」與「保障」並行，別讓「拓寬選擇」變成「降低標準」。  
• 對開發商與營運者：以「社群」而非「填滿床位」決勝。投資住戶體驗團隊、透明的住戶公約，以及讓「共享」感覺像特色而非妥協的設計。推出高齡與跨世代模式，而不只服務年輕專業人士。  
• 對社會住宅供應者與非營利組織：為市場忽視的族群試辦共居——固定收入的長者、離開安置體系的青年、脫離無家狀態者。共享居住加上輕度支持，可能比孤立的單元更便宜也更有人味。  
• 對租客與社區：把共居視為一個正當的人生階段，而非最後手段。在地倡議放寬過時的入住人數上限——它們正默默讓數以百萬計的臥室空置，而租金節節高升。

更深層的訊息是：住房危機無法僅靠「蓋房子」解決。最有力的「新住房」，有時正是我們既有的空間——靠更好的規則、更好的管理，以及一份願意分享的意願來釋放。

Title: Can a Documentary Heal? Matthew Perry, Netflix, and the Social Innovation of Storytelling

Introduction  
On October 27, Netflix released The One About Matthew Perry, a three-part documentary series directed by Mary Robertson that traces the beloved Friends star's career, his decades-long battle with addiction, his death in 2023, and the criminal investigation that followed. Built around intimate interviews—his sister Mia Perry Bowick is a central voice, alongside close friends and former NBC executives—the series became one of the week's most-discussed cultural events. It arrives alongside the Matthew Perry Foundation, established within days of his death to help people struggling with addiction. On the surface this is celebrity entertainment. But viewed through a social-innovation lens, a mass-audience documentary about addiction is something more: a piece of cultural infrastructure that can shift stigma, norms and behavior at a scale clinical services rarely reach.

The Social Innovation Angle  
Social innovation is often imagined as apps, co-ops or policy. But narrative is one of the oldest and most powerful social technologies—and a documentary watched by tens of millions is a distribution channel most public-health campaigns can only envy.  
1\. Storytelling as destigmatization at scale. Addiction's deadliest comorbidity is shame, which keeps people from seeking help. By letting a familiar, loved figure be shown as a person who struggled—not a cautionary villain—the documentary can reframe addiction as a treatable health condition rather than a moral failure. This "contact-based" change in public attitudes is a documented mechanism: humanizing stories reduce stigma more effectively than statistics alone.  
2\. The celebrity foundation as a social-finance vehicle. The Matthew Perry Foundation, stewarded by the National Philanthropic Trust, channels public grief into sustained funding for recovery. The innovation is not the charity itself but the flywheel: a cultural moment (the documentary) drives attention, attention drives donations and awareness, and these fund peer-support and recovery services. When designed well, pop culture becomes a renewable fundraising and recruitment engine for a cause.  
3\. From passive viewing to active pathways. The frontier question is whether a documentary converts emotion into action. The most socially innovative releases now pair content with "next steps"—helpline numbers, peer-recovery communities, screening tools—embedded at the moment of peak empathy. Social innovation here means handling a real person's tragedy with care, and ensuring the "call to action" leads to services that actually exist.

Actionable Insights and Recommendations  
• For storytellers and platforms: Design the "last frame" deliberately. End episodes with concrete, localized pathways—helplines, peer-recovery groups, how to support someone—so empathy becomes action while it is fresh. Treat the content and the care infrastructure as one product.

引言  
10 月 27 日，Netflix 上線三集紀錄片《The One About Matthew Perry》，由 Mary Robertson 執導，爬梳這位《六人行》（Friends）人氣演員的演藝生涯、長達數十年的成癮之戰、2023 年的離世，以及其後的刑事調查。影集以親密訪談為核心——他的妹妹 Mia Perry Bowick 是主要敘事者，另有摯友與前 NBC 高層——迅速成為本週最受熱議的文化事件之一。它與「馬修派瑞基金會」（Matthew Perry Foundation，在他離世數日內成立，協助成癮受苦者）相互呼應。表面上，這是名人娛樂；但透過社會創新的透鏡來看，一部面向大眾、談論成癮的紀錄片，其實是更重要的東西：一種能在臨床服務難以觸及的規模上，撼動污名、規範與行為的「文化基礎設施」。

社會創新角度分析  
社會創新常被想像成 App、合作社或政策；但「敘事」是最古老、也最強大的社會技術之一——而一部數千萬人觀看的紀錄片，正是多數公共衛生宣導難以企及的傳播通路。  
一、以規模化敘事去除污名。成癮最致命的「共病」是羞恥感，它讓人不敢求助。當一位熟悉而受愛戴的人物，被呈現為「曾經掙扎的人」而非警世的惡角，紀錄片便能把成癮重新框定為「可治療的健康狀況」，而非「道德缺陷」。這種「接觸式」的態度轉變有實證機制：人性化的故事，比單純的統計數字更能有效降低污名。  
二、名人基金會作為一種「社會金融」載具。由 National Philanthropic Trust 託管的馬修派瑞基金會，把公眾的哀悼導向對康復服務的持續資助。創新之處不在慈善本身，而在那個「飛輪」：一個文化時刻（紀錄片）帶動關注，關注帶動捐款與意識，而這些又資助同儕支持與康復服務。設計得當時，流行文化就成為某項志業可再生的募款與招募引擎。  
三、從被動觀看到主動路徑。前沿的問題是：紀錄片能否把情緒轉化為行動？最具社會創新性的作品，如今會把「下一步」與內容綁在一起——求助專線、同儕康復社群、自我篩檢工具——並嵌入在同理心最高的那一刻。派瑞本人長年倡議同儕支持（康復中的人去幫助他人，具有獨特成效）；一部能把觀眾導向這類社群的紀錄片，就把「兩小時的眼淚」變成「一道門」。  
其中也有真實張力：以「真實犯罪」框架處理一個人的死亡，可能滑向獵奇；哀悼可能被商品化；而「只有意識、卻無可負擔治療管道」，可能燃起無法兌現的希望。此處的社會創新，意味著以謹慎對待一個真實之人的悲劇，並確保「行動呼籲」導向確實存在的服務。

可行啟示與行動建議  
• 對說故事者與平台：刻意設計「最後一格畫面」。在每集結尾放上具體、在地化的路徑——求助專線、同儕康復團體、如何陪伴身邊的人——讓同理心在最新鮮時轉為行動。把「內容」與「關懷基礎設施」當成同一個產品。  
• 對醫療體系與非營利組織：乘上文化浪潮。在重大作品上線前後預先部署量能（專線人力、初談名額、同儕導師招募），因為每當這類故事問世，搜尋與來電都會可預期地暴增。  
• 對基金會與資助者：打造「飛輪」，而非僅是「基金」。把一次性、哀悼驅動的捐款轉為定期支持，並投資低成本、高信任、可擴展的同儕康復模式，而不只是高成本的臨床量能。  
• 對觀眾與社區：善用這個時刻，關心一位朋友、學習康復的語言，並把成癮視為健康議題。這類故事最有力的結果，往往是一場餐桌上的對話——它替某個人通往求助的路，卸下了羞恥。

娛樂很少「只是」娛樂。當數百萬人一同哀悼一位共同的文化人物，那份集體情緒是一種稀有的公共資源。社會創新的任務，就是把它轉化為能持久的東西：更少的污名、更多的路徑，以及準備好迎接「被故事送來求助的人」的服務。
