### **English Draft**

**Title: When Restaurants Stop Wasting: Zero-Waste Dining as a Social-Innovation Playbook**  
**Introduction.** For one week at the turn of October, some of New York's best kitchens competed not on luxury but on leaving nothing behind. Mill's third annual "Make Food Not Waste Restaurant Week" (September 28–October 4\) asked chefs to design menus that treat the whole ingredient as food: Corima served a bluefin tostada topped with a sikil pak made from mango seeds, while Blue Hill turned leftovers into fish-scale rice crackers. The timing was deliberate, overlapping with national Food Waste Prevention Week. Behind the playful dishes sits a sobering number from ReFED: the U.S. foodservice sector alone generates roughly 12.5 million tons of surplus food a year, worth about \$157 billion—around 14% of sales—and some 78% of it still goes to landfill. The story went viral because it reframed an abstract climate statistic as something you could taste on a plate.  
**The social-innovation lens.** Social innovation is not simply a clever product; it is a new combination of ideas, relationships and incentives that solves a social problem more effectively than existing arrangements, and that can spread. Zero-waste dining qualifies on three counts. First, it reframes the problem: waste stops being an unavoidable cost of hospitality and becomes a design flaw to be engineered out, menu by menu. Second, it rewires relationships—chefs, compost haulers (Mill partnered with Reclaimed Organics), community gardens that receive the finished compost, and diners who become participants rather than spectators. Third, it builds a replicable model: a themed "week," a scoring logic (how little can you waste?), and shareable dishes that other restaurants can copy. Alongside this sits a parallel innovation in the consumer market—surplus-food marketplaces such as Too Good To Go, whose "surprise bag" lets shops sell unsold food cheaply at day's end instead of binning it. Together they show two complementary modes of social innovation: a values-led, chef-driven cultural movement, and a platform that makes the sustainable choice the economically rational one.  
**Why it matters beyond the plate.** The deeper move is a shift from a linear "buy–cook–discard" system to a circular one, where yesterday's trimmings become today's garnish and tomorrow's compost. This is the circular economy made legible at human scale. It also redistributes value: money that once literally went into the bin is recaptured as margin for the business, affordability for the diner, and nutrients for the soil. Critically, the model attacks waste at the point of generation rather than downstream. Most food-waste policy historically focused on diversion—getting scraps out of landfill—but prevention (not generating the surplus in the first place) sits higher on the waste hierarchy and delivers far larger climate returns per dollar. A restaurant week that trains chefs to plan portions, use the whole animal or vegetable, and track waste is, in effect, a behaviour-change intervention disguised as a culinary festival.  
**Limits and honest caveats.** A one-week event risks being performative if kitchens revert to old habits on day eight; visibility is not the same as durable change. Zero-waste menus can also demand skilled labour and tight supply chains that smaller or lower-margin restaurants struggle to afford. And surplus-food apps, while useful, can unintentionally encourage over-production if businesses treat the discount channel as a safety valve. The innovation only compounds if measurement and incentives persist after the cameras leave.  
**Actionable takeaways.**  
1\. *For restaurants and caterers:* run a two-week waste audit before redesigning menus; the data almost always reveals that 3–5 ingredients drive most waste. Set a public reduction target and report against it—accountability is what turns a stunt into a system.  
2\. *For cities and trade associations:* convert the "restaurant week" format into an annual, judged competition with a simple shared metric (waste per cover). Pair it with subsidised compost collection so prevention and diversion reinforce each other.  
3\. *For platforms and funders:* reward prevention, not just redistribution—design surplus-food marketplaces so that falling surplus (not just more bags sold) is the headline success metric.  
4\. *For diners and schools:* treat "eat the whole thing" as a teachable skill. A classroom or household that learns to cook stems, peels and bones replicates, cheaply, what star chefs demonstrated this week.  
5\. *For everyone:* the transferable principle is to attack problems at the source and redesign the system, rather than managing the symptom at the end of the pipe.

### **繁體中文初稿**

**標題：當餐廳不再浪費──把「零廚餘」變成一套可複製的社會創新劇本**  
**引言。**十月初的一週，紐約幾家頂尖餐廳比的不是奢華，而是「什麼都不剩」。由永續品牌 Mill 主辦、邁入第三屆的「Make Food Not Waste 零廚餘餐廳週」（9/28–10/4）邀請主廚設計「整株、整尾皆是食材」的菜單：Corima 端出以芒果籽製成 sikil pak 醬的藍鰭鮪魚脆餅，Blue Hill 則把剩料做成魚鱗米餅。活動刻意與全美「惜食預防週」重疊。這些俏皮料理背後，是 ReFED 一組令人清醒的數字：光是美國餐飲業，每年產生約 1,250 萬噸剩食、價值約 1,570 億美元（約佔營業額 14%），其中約 78% 仍進了掩埋場。它之所以在網路上發酵，是因為把抽象的氣候數據，變成了你盤中吃得到的味道。  
**社會創新的視角。**社會創新不只是一個聰明的產品，而是把想法、關係與誘因重新組合，比既有做法更有效地解決社會問題，而且能擴散。「零廚餘餐飲」在三個層面符合這個定義。其一，它重新定義問題：浪費不再是餐飲業無可避免的成本，而是一個可以「設計掉」的缺陷，一道菜一道菜地被工程化地消除。其二，它重接關係網：主廚、廚餘回收業者（Mill 與 Reclaimed Organics 合作）、接收成品堆肥的社區農園，以及從旁觀者變成參與者的食客。其三，它建立可複製的模式：一個有主題的「週」、一套計分邏輯（你能少浪費多少？），以及其他餐廳能照抄的料理。與之平行的，是消費端的另一種創新──像 Too Good To Go 這類「惜食市集」，用「驚喜袋」讓店家在打烊前把賣不完的食物低價售出，而非丟棄。兩者展示了社會創新互補的兩種路徑：一種是價值導向、由主廚帶動的文化運動；另一種是讓「永續選擇」同時成為「經濟上最划算選擇」的平台機制。  
**為何意義超越餐盤。**更深層的轉變，是從「購買—烹調—丟棄」的線性系統，走向「昨日的邊角料成為今日的點綴、明日的堆肥」的循環系統。這是把循環經濟縮小到人人看得懂的生活尺度。它同時重新分配價值：原本字面上「丟進垃圾桶的錢」，被重新回收為店家的利潤、食客的可負擔性，以及土壤的養分。關鍵在於，這套模式是在「產生浪費的源頭」出手，而非下游處理。過去多數惜食政策聚焦於「分流」──把廚餘帶離掩埋場；但「預防」（根本不要產生剩食）在廢棄物管理層級中位階更高，每一塊錢帶來的減碳效益也遠大得多。一個教主廚規劃份量、善用整隻動物或整顆蔬菜、並追蹤浪費的餐廳週，本質上是一場「偽裝成美食節的行為改變介入」。  
**誠實的限制。**若廚房在第八天就回到舊習慣，一週活動恐淪為表演；「被看見」不等於「持續改變」。零廚餘菜單往往需要熟練人力與緊密供應鏈，對規模小、毛利薄的餐廳是負擔。惜食 App 雖好，若店家把折扣通路當成「安全閥」，也可能反而鼓勵過度生產。唯有在鏡頭離開後，量測與誘因仍持續，創新才會累積複利。  
**可行的啟示與行動建議。**  
1\.*餐廳與外燴業者：*重新設計菜單前，先做兩週廚餘稽核；數據幾乎總會顯示，是 3–5 項食材造成了多數浪費。設定公開的減量目標並對外揭露進度──問責機制，才是把「噱頭」變成「系統」的關鍵。  
2\.*城市與公協會：*把「餐廳週」升級為每年評審、採用同一簡單指標（每客廚餘量）的競賽，並搭配補助式廚餘回收，讓「預防」與「分流」相互強化。  
3\.*平台與出資者：*獎勵「預防」而非僅「再分配」──把惜食市集的首要成功指標，設定為「剩食總量下降」，而不只是「賣出更多驚喜袋」。  
4\.*食客與學校：*把「整株吃完」當成可教的技能。學會料理菜梗、果皮與骨頭的家庭或課堂，就能用低成本複製明星主廚本週示範的事。  
5\.*給所有人：*可遷移的原則是──在源頭解決問題、重新設計系統，而不是在管線末端處理症狀。

### **English Draft**

**Title: The Spare-Room Revolution: Co-Living as Social Innovation in the Housing Crisis**  
**Introduction.** This week the housing conversation shifted from "build more" to "use what we already have." A widely shared Black Enterprise report (October 1\) spotlighted co-living as a fast-growing answer to unaffordable rents, with operators and lawmakers eyeing the millions of under-used bedrooms sitting empty in existing homes. One operator, PadSplit, says it has housed more than 70,000 people across some 30 markets in rooms that cost substantially less than a conventional apartment. In Melbourne, co-living rooms are advertised at around A\$380 a week against A\$550–650 for a studio. But the model collided with an old legal wall: a Pacific Legal Foundation analysis found most of Pennsylvania's largest cities cap how many unrelated adults may share a home, and in Shawnee, Kansas a federal judge dismissed a challenge to such a rule, leaning on a 1974 Supreme Court precedent. The result is a live, national argument about who gets to live together, and where.  
**The social-innovation lens.** Co-living is a textbook case of social innovation because it creates new value from an existing, idle asset—the spare bedroom—much as ride-hailing did with idle cars. The innovation is not the building; it is the reconfiguration of a resource plus a new set of rules and services (furnished rooms, utilities bundled, flexible leases, light-touch management) that make shared living workable for strangers. It also widens who the market serves. The Black Enterprise framing is significant: co-living is increasingly pitched not at twenty-something tech workers but at older adults on fixed incomes, for whom a shared home offers both affordability and companionship—addressing two social problems, housing cost and isolation, at once. That dual payoff—economic and relational—is a hallmark of strong social innovation.  
**Why it matters beyond rent.** The deeper significance is that co-living exposes how much of the housing crisis is a regulatory and design problem rather than purely a construction-cost problem. We have the bedrooms; what we lack is permission and the social infrastructure to share them well. That reframing matters because adding supply by converting existing space is faster, cheaper and lower-carbon than new construction. It also surfaces a genuine tension at the heart of social innovation: rules written for one era (single-family zoning, "unrelated adult" caps from the 1970s) can quietly block the solutions a new era needs. The Kansas and Pennsylvania fights are, in effect, a negotiation over whether our legal definition of a "household" should expand to match how people actually live—older adults pooling resources, remote workers seeking community, newcomers needing an affordable first foothold.  
**Limits and honest caveats.** Co-living is not a universal fix. Where it is run purely as a yield play, it can degrade into overcrowding, thin tenant protections and the quiet erosion of neighbourhood character that fuels local opposition. Shared living demands governance—conflict resolution, cleaning norms, safety—that platforms do not automatically provide. And "unrelated adult" caps, however outdated, were sometimes written to prevent exploitative rooming houses; reform must protect tenants, not just unlock supply. The innovation succeeds only if affordability and dignity move together.  
**Actionable takeaways.**  
1\. *For city governments:* audit and modernise "unrelated adult" occupancy caps and single-family zoning; replace blanket bans with health-and-safety standards (egress, space per person, registration) that permit sharing while preventing exploitation.  
2\. *For operators and non-profits:* design explicitly for intergenerational and older-adult co-living, bundling light support services; measure loneliness and housing stability, not just occupancy.  
3\. *For communities and faith groups:* treat the spare room as civic infrastructure—home-share matching programs (pairing an older homeowner with a vetted younger tenant) are a low-cost, high-trust version of the same idea.  
4\. *For tenants:* insist on written agreements covering rent, exit, privacy and dispute resolution; the flexibility that makes co-living attractive also makes clear rules essential.  
5\. *For everyone:* the transferable lesson is to look first for idle assets and outdated rules before assuming a problem requires expensive new building.

### **繁體中文初稿**

**標題：閒置房間革命──用「共居」為居住危機尋找社會創新解方**  
**引言。**本週，住房討論從「蓋更多」轉向「善用既有」。一篇被廣為轉傳的 Black Enterprise 報導（10/1）聚焦「共居（co-living）」作為高房租的快速解方：業者與立法者把目光投向現有住宅中數以百萬計閒置的臥房。其中一家業者 PadSplit 表示，它已在約 30 個市場、讓逾 7 萬人入住價格明顯低於傳統公寓的房間。在墨爾本，共居房間週租約 380 澳元，傳統套房則要 550–650 澳元。但這套模式撞上一道老舊的法律高牆：太平洋法律基金會分析指出，賓州多數大城市限制「非親屬成年人」同住人數；在堪薩斯州 Shawnee，一名聯邦法官更援引 1974 年最高法院判例，駁回對此類限制的挑戰。於是，一場關於「誰能和誰、在哪裡同住」的全國性辯論就此點燃。  
**社會創新的視角。**共居是社會創新的教科書案例，因為它從一項既有、閒置的資產──空房間──創造出新價值，正如共乘平台活化了閒置車輛。創新的核心不是建築本身，而是「資源的重新配置」加上一套新的規則與服務（附家具的房間、水電網路包租、彈性租約、輕度管理），讓陌生人共同生活變得可行。它也擴大了市場所服務的對象。Black Enterprise 的切入點意義重大：共居愈來愈不是為二十多歲科技業者設計，而是瞄準收入固定的「中高齡長者」──對他們而言，共享住宅同時提供「可負擔」與「陪伴」，一次處理「住房成本」與「孤獨」兩個社會問題。這種「經濟＋關係」的雙重效益，正是強韌社會創新的註冊商標。  
**為何意義超越房租。**更深層的意義在於，共居揭露了住房危機有很大一部分是「法規與設計」問題，而非單純的「營建成本」問題。我們其實有房間，缺的是「許可」與「把房間分享好的社會基礎設施」。這個重新框定很重要：透過改造既有空間來增加供給，比新建更快、更省、碳排更低。它也點出社會創新核心的一個真實張力：為某個時代寫就的規則（單一家庭分區、1970 年代的「非親屬成年人」人數上限）會悄悄擋住新時代所需的解方。堪薩斯與賓州的角力，實質上是在協商：我們對「家戶」的法律定義，是否該擴大以符合人們真實的居住方式──長者共同分攤資源、遠距工作者尋求社群、新移民需要一個可負擔的落腳起點。  
**誠實的限制。**共居並非萬靈丹。若純粹被當成收益操作，它可能惡化為過度擁擠、租客保障單薄，並悄悄侵蝕社區紋理、進而激起在地反對。共同生活需要「治理」──衝突調解、清潔規範、安全管理──而這些平台不會自動提供。此外，「非親屬成年人」上限雖然老舊，有時當初正是為了防止剝削式的分租屋；改革必須同時保護租客，而不只是釋放供給。唯有「可負擔」與「尊嚴」一起前進，創新才算成功。  
**可行的啟示與行動建議。**  
1\.*城市政府：*盤點並現代化「非親屬成年人」入住上限與單一家庭分區；以「健康與安全標準」（逃生動線、人均空間、登記制）取代一刀切禁令，既允許共享、又防止剝削。  
2\.*業者與非營利組織：*明確為「跨世代與長者共居」設計，搭配輕度支持服務；衡量指標應納入「孤獨感」與「居住穩定度」，而不只是「入住率」。  
3\.*社區與信仰團體：*把空房間視為公民基礎設施──「住房媒合」計畫（把有空房的年長屋主，與經審核的年輕租客配對）是同一構想中低成本、高信任的版本。  
4\.*租客：*堅持簽訂涵蓋租金、退租、隱私與爭議處理的書面協議；讓共居有吸引力的「彈性」，也正因此更需要「清楚的規則」。  
5\.*給所有人：*可遷移的教訓是──在假設問題「需要昂貴新建」之前，先尋找「閒置的資產」與「過時的規則」。

### **English Draft**

**Title: Rolling Toward the 15-Minute City: Shared Micromobility as Social Innovation**  
**Introduction.** The "15-minute city"—the idea that daily needs should sit within a short walk or ride of home—is back in the spotlight, and this time the debate is empirical rather than utopian. A 2026 study in the Journal of Geographical Systems examines whether shared e-bikes and e-scooters actually expand the 15-minute city, acknowledging that their real contribution is still poorly understood. Industry trackers report that North American shared micromobility has hit peak ridership, with roughly 79% of systems now offering electric vehicles and about two-thirds of all trips taken on e-devices. Meanwhile regulators are drawing lines: Beijing's non-motor-vehicle rules now mandate helmets, license plates valid for a decade, and caps on shared fleets. The conversation has matured from "are e-scooters a nuisance?" to "how do we design shared mobility so a city's opportunities reach everyone within fifteen minutes?"  
**The social-innovation lens.** Shared micromobility is social innovation because it unbundles mobility from ownership. Instead of each resident buying and storing a vehicle, a shared fleet turns transport into an on-demand service, lowering the cost of reaching jobs, shops and clinics—especially for people who cannot afford a car. Paired with the 15-minute-city concept, it targets a structural inequity: in car-dependent cities, access to opportunity is effectively rationed by who can drive. The innovation is systemic rather than gadget-level; it combines a technology (light electric vehicles), a service model (dockless or docked sharing, increasingly bundled into Mobility-as-a-Service apps), and an urban-design philosophy (dense, mixed-use neighbourhoods) into a single approach to a social problem—unequal access and transport emissions. The "E-Bike City" proposal goes further, reallocating road space away from cars toward active travel and transit, which is as much a political and distributive intervention as a technical one.  
**Why it matters beyond the commute.** Transport is a determinant of life chances: the ability to reach a job interview, a school, or a hospital on time shapes income, health and inclusion. A city organised around fifteen-minute access, served by affordable shared vehicles, can widen those chances while cutting the roughly quarter of urban emissions that come from transport. There is also a "last-mile" multiplier: micromobility feeds public transit, extending the catchment of every bus and train station without laying new track. Crucially, the 2026 research signals a healthy maturation—innovators are now asking for evidence rather than assuming benefits. That evidentiary turn is itself part of responsible social innovation: scaling what is proven to work and equitable, rather than what is merely novel.  
**Limits and honest caveats.** Shared micromobility can deepen inequity if it clusters in affluent districts and skips lower-income or peripheral neighbourhoods—exactly where car-dependence bites hardest. Safety and sidewalk clutter are real concerns, which is why Beijing's helmet and fleet-cap rules matter. And the 15-minute city only works if it is paired with genuinely mixed-use, affordable neighbourhoods; otherwise it risks becoming an amenity for the already well-served, or a target for conspiracy-tinged backlash. Equity of deployment, not just existence of service, is the test.  
**Actionable takeaways.**  
1\. *For cities:* mandate equitable deployment in shared-mobility contracts—require operators to serve lower-income and peripheral zones, with discounted fares for residents on assistance, as a condition of the permit.  
2\. *For transit agencies:* integrate micromobility into one ticket and one app (Mobility-as-a-Service) so a bike-to-bus-to-train trip is seamless; fund protected lanes and parking corrals to make it safe and tidy.  
3\. *For planners:* pursue the 15-minute city through incremental mixed-use rezoning and "complete streets," and publish access metrics (what share of residents can reach daily needs in 15 minutes) to hold the policy accountable.  
4\. *For employers and schools:* subsidise shared-mobility passes the way parking is subsidised today, shifting the default away from the private car.  
5\. *For everyone:* the transferable principle is to turn ownership into access, and to measure a system by whether its benefits reach the least-served, not the average user.

### **繁體中文初稿**

**標題：滑向「15 分鐘城市」──把共享微型運具當成一種社會創新**  
**引言。**「15 分鐘城市」──日常所需都在住家步行或短程車距之內──再度成為焦點，而這一次的辯論是「用證據說話」，而非烏托邦式空想。2026 年《Journal of Geographical Systems》一篇研究，檢視共享電動自行車與電動滑板車是否真能擴展 15 分鐘城市，並坦承其實際貢獻仍不甚明朗。產業追蹤報告指出，北美共享微型運具已達載客量高峰，約 79% 的系統提供電動車款、約三分之二的行程以電動運具完成。與此同時，監理方也在劃線：北京新版非機動車規定要求所有騎士配戴安全帽、電動自行車掛牌（效期十年），並對共享車隊總量設上限。討論已從「電動滑板車是不是麻煩製造者？」，成熟到「我們該如何設計共享運具，讓城市的機會在 15 分鐘內觸及每一個人？」  
**社會創新的視角。**共享微型運具之所以是社會創新，是因為它把「移動」從「擁有」中解綁。居民不必人人購買並停放一台車，共享車隊把交通變成隨需服務，降低了抵達工作、商店與診所的成本──尤其對買不起汽車的人而言。再與 15 分鐘城市概念結合，它瞄準了一個結構性的不公：在高度依賴汽車的城市裡，「接近機會的能力」實際上是依「誰能開車」來分配的。這項創新屬於系統層次，而非單一裝置：它把一項技術（輕型電動載具）、一套服務模式（無樁或有樁共享，並日益整合進「行動即服務／MaaS」App），以及一套都市設計哲學（高密度、混合使用的鄰里），整合成對同一社會問題（機會不均與運輸排碳）的單一解方。而「E-Bike City」的構想更進一步，把道路空間從汽車手中重新分配給主動運具與大眾運輸──這既是技術介入，更是政治與分配上的介入。  
**為何意義超越通勤。**交通是「人生機會」的決定因素之一：能否準時抵達一場面試、一所學校、一間醫院，形塑著收入、健康與社會參與。一座以「15 分鐘可達」為骨架、由可負擔共享運具服務的城市，能在拓寬這些機會的同時，削減佔都市排碳約四分之一的運輸排放。它還有「最後一哩」的乘數效果：微型運具餵養大眾運輸，在不鋪設新軌道的前提下，擴大每一個公車與捷運站的服務範圍。更關鍵的是，2026 年的研究透露出一種健康的成熟──創新者開始「要證據」，而非理所當然地假設有好處。這種轉向「以證據為本」，本身就是負責任社會創新的一部分：擴大「已被證明有效且公平」的做法，而非只是「新奇」的做法。  
**誠實的限制。**若共享微型運具只集中在富裕城區、略過低收入或邊陲社區，反而會加深不公──而那些地方正是最受汽車依賴之苦的所在。安全與人行道堆置是真實的隱憂，這也是北京安全帽與車隊上限規定的意義所在。此外，15 分鐘城市唯有搭配「真正混合使用、可負擔」的鄰里才行得通；否則可能淪為「服務本已充足者」的加碼福利，或成為陰謀論式反彈的箭靶。檢驗標準是「部署的公平性」，而不只是「服務存在與否」。  
**可行的啟示與行動建議。**  
1\.*城市政府：*在共享運具特許合約中明訂「公平部署」──要求業者必須服務低收入與邊陲區域，並對領取社福補助的居民提供折扣費率，作為發照條件。  
2\.*運輸主管機關：*把微型運具整合進「一票、一 App」（MaaS），讓「單車轉公車轉捷運」無縫銜接；並出資興建受保護車道與停放區，兼顧安全與整潔。  
3\.*規劃者：*以漸進式的混合使用重新分區與「完整街道」推動 15 分鐘城市，並公布「可及性指標」（多少比例居民能在 15 分鐘內滿足日常所需），讓政策可被問責。  
4\.*雇主與學校：*像今天補貼停車位一樣補貼共享運具月票，把預設選項從私家車移開。  
5\.*給所有人：*可遷移的原則是──把「擁有」轉為「取用」，並以「好處是否觸及最少被服務的人」而非「平均使用者」，來評斷一套系統的成敗。
