### **English Draft**

#### **When a Nation Rallies Around a Home: Spain's Housing Uprising and the Social-Innovation Playbook for Affordable Living**

**Introduction.** This week the internet could not look away from Spain, where the eviction of 87-year-old Maricarmen Abascal from the Madrid apartment she had lived in for some seven decades became the spark for one of Europe's largest housing mobilisations in years. Tens of thousands marched; protest camps appeared in Puerta del Sol and in Barcelona; lawmakers rejected an emergency package that would have limited evictions, curbed speculation and offered renters indefinite leases; and the prime minister called a snap election. A single grandmother on a stretcher turned an abstract macro-statistic — unaffordable housing — into a story everyone shared. Behind the political theatre, however, sits a question that is squarely in the domain of social innovation: how do societies actually produce homes that ordinary people can afford to keep?  
**The social-innovation lens.** Housing stress is not merely a shortage of buildings; it is a failure of the institutions that allocate, price and govern shelter. Social innovation reframes the problem away from "build more / regulate more" binaries and toward new social arrangements for ownership, tenure and risk-sharing. Spain's crisis is instructive precisely because the market-only and state-only responses have both visibly strained — three-quarters of Spaniards now say the state should intervene "as necessary," yet markets alone keep pricing residents out. The interesting space is the civic and cooperative middle.  
Three families of innovation are worth naming. First, *community land trusts (CLTs)*, in which a non-profit permanently holds the land and sells or rents only the building, decoupling the home from land speculation and locking in affordability across generations. Second, *cohousing and tenant cooperatives* such as Barcelona's celebrated La Borda, where residents collectively finance, design and govern their building on public land under long leases — homes treated as a right of use rather than a tradeable asset. Third, *tenant unions and digital organising*: Spain's Sindicato de Inquilinas has turned fragmented, frightened renters into a coordinated bloc capable of blocking evictions building by building and shaping national policy. Each is a social technology, not merely a financial one; each changes *who decides* and *who benefits*.  
What makes these approaches "innovative" rather than nostalgic is their blending of old mutualism with new tools. Digital platforms let tenant unions map evictions in real time, crowdfund legal defence, and mobilise flash solidarity at a threatened doorstep within hours. Blended-finance structures let CLTs combine philanthropic capital, patient public loans and resident equity so that affordability survives the next market cycle. The Spanish moment shows both the necessity of these models and the political window that crises open for them.  
**Risks and honest limits.** Social innovation in housing is not a silver bullet. CLTs and co-ops scale slowly and depend on access to cheap land — usually public land that governments must be willing to release. Price controls can suppress new supply if used bluntly. And movements born of anger can fracture once the immediate threat recedes. A mature reading treats these models as complements to, not substitutes for, large-scale public and social housing investment.  
**Implications and recommended actions.**

> * **For city governments:** release idle public land on 75–99 year leases specifically to CLTs and cooperatives, and create a one-stop "affordable tenure" office that helps resident groups navigate finance and planning.  
> * **For philanthropy and impact investors:** fund the patient, pre-development capital that co-ops cannot raise from banks — the single biggest bottleneck in every country that has tried this.  
> * **For civic technologists:** build open-source tooling for tenant unions — eviction-mapping, rights chatbots, collective-bargaining dashboards — and make it portable across cities.  
> * **For readers and residents:** treat housing as infrastructure for life, not an asset class; support a local tenant union or housing co-op before a crisis arrives at your own door.

The image of Maricarmen will fade from feeds within days. The structural question she embodied will not. Whether Spain — and the many countries watching — converts this week's outrage into durable new institutions is the real test of social innovation.

#### **當一個國家為「一個家」走上街頭：西班牙居住正義運動與可負擔居住的社會創新解方**

**引言。**本週互聯網的目光難以離開西班牙。87 歲的瑪麗卡門（Maricarmen Abascal）被迫搬離她住了約七十年的馬德里公寓，這一幕成為近年歐洲最大規模居住運動的導火線：數萬人上街，太陽門廣場與巴塞隆納出現抗議營地；國會否決了一套包含限制驅離、抑制炒作、給予租客不定期租約的緊急方案；首相隨後宣布提前大選。一位被抬上擔架的長者，把「買不起、租不起房」這個抽象的總體數據，變成了全民轉傳的故事。然而在政治喧囂背後，有一個正屬於社會創新範疇的提問：社會究竟要如何「生產」出一般人住得起、也守得住的家？  
**社會創新的視角。**居住壓力並非單純的房子不夠，而是分配、定價與治理住所的制度失靈。社會創新把問題從「多蓋房 vs. 多管制」的二元對立，轉移到關於所有權、使用權與風險分擔的「新社會安排」上。西班牙之所以值得借鏡，正因為純市場與純國家兩種路徑都明顯吃緊——如今四分之三的西班牙人認為國家應「在必要時」介入，但單靠市場又持續把居民擠出城市。真正有趣的，是公民與合作社構成的「中間地帶」。  
值得點名的有三類創新。其一是*社區土地信託（CLT）*：由非營利組織永久持有土地、只出售或出租地上的建物，使「住宅」與「土地炒作」脫鉤，讓可負擔性能跨世代鎖定。其二是*共居與租客合作社*，例如巴塞隆納著名的 La Borda——居民在公有地上以長期租約，集體出資、設計並治理自己的住宅，把家當成「使用權」而非可交易的資產。其三是*租客工會與數位串連*：西班牙的租客工會把原本零散、恐懼的租屋族組織成能逐棟阻止驅離、並影響國家政策的力量。這三者都是「社會技術」而不僅是金融工具，因為它們改變了「誰來決定」與「誰能受益」。  
這些做法之所以是「創新」而非懷舊，在於它把古老的互助精神與新工具結合：數位平台讓租客工會能即時繪製驅離地圖、群眾募資法律辯護，並在數小時內於受威脅的門前集結聲援；混合式金融則讓 CLT 結合公益資本、耐心的公共貸款與居民權益股，使可負擔性撐過下一輪市場循環。西班牙此刻既顯示了這些模式的必要性，也打開了危機才會出現的政策窗口。  
**風險與誠實的限制。**居住領域的社會創新並非萬靈丹。CLT 與合作社規模擴張緩慢，且高度依賴取得廉價土地——通常是政府須願意釋出的公有地。粗糙的租金管制可能抑制新供給；而因憤怒而生的運動，也可能在威脅退去後分裂。成熟的判讀應把這些模式視為大規模公共與社會住宅投資的「互補」，而非「替代」。  
**啟示與可行行動建議。**

> * **給城市政府：**以 75–99 年長期租約，專門向 CLT 與合作社釋出閒置公有地，並設立「可負擔居住」單一窗口，協助居民團體處理融資與都市規劃。  
> * **給公益與影響力投資者：**補上合作社向銀行募不到的「開發前耐心資本」——這是每個嘗試過此路徑的國家最大的瓶頸。  
> * **給公民科技工作者：**為租客工會打造開源工具（驅離地圖、權利聊天機器人、集體協商儀表板），並讓它能跨城市移植。  
> * **給讀者與居民：**把居住視為「生活的基礎設施」而非資產類別；在危機尚未降臨自家門前時，先支持在地的租客工會或住宅合作社。

瑪麗卡門的身影幾天內就會從動態消息淡出，但她所體現的結構性提問不會。西班牙——以及眾多觀望的國家——能否把本週的憤怒轉化為可長可久的新制度，才是社會創新真正的考題。

**來源連結：** [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/holidays/halloween/most-popular-halloween-costumes-2026-ranked)｜ [NJBIZ（Spirit Halloween 2026）](https://njbiz.com/spirit-halloween-2026-costume-trends/)｜ [Netflix Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/kpop-demon-hunters-disguise-costumes)｜ [NW Asian Weekly](https://nwasianweekly.com/2025/10/aapi-culture-dominates-halloween-costume-trends-led-by-kpop-demon-hunters)

### **English Draft**

#### **HUNTR/X Everywhere: What the KPop Demon Hunters Costume Craze Reveals About Culture, Consumption, and Circular Fashion**

**Introduction.** As Halloween approaches, one costume is eating the internet: the HUNTR/X looks from *KPop Demon Hunters*. More than 200,000 people Googled the costume in September alone; it tops Pinterest and Spirit Halloween trend reports; and it is the rare pick that has now dominated two consecutive seasons. Americans are projected to spend roughly US\$13.5 billion on Halloween in 2026, and Spirit Halloween opened a record 1,575 stores to meet the rush. On the surface this is a merchandising story. Seen through a social-innovation lens, it is three stories: about whose culture gets to be mainstream, about how fans co-create value, and about the quiet environmental scandal of disposable dress-up.  
**The social-innovation lens.** Social innovation is usually associated with housing or health, not Halloween. But culture is where norms are made, and norms are the raw material of social change. The costume boom matters for three reasons.  
First, *representation as innovation*. A franchise rooted in Korean pop culture becoming the default costume for American kids and pets is not trivial; commentators noted that AAPI culture is now leading mainstream Halloween trends. When a child of any background chooses to become Rumi or Zoey, a cultural hierarchy quietly shifts. Media that normalises previously marginalised cultures is a form of social innovation in the "diffusion of new norms" sense — it changes what feels ordinary.  
Second, *participatory and fan-driven value*. The craze is not manufactured top-down; it is amplified by fans making their own versions, tutorials and remixes. This is the creator-economy logic: the audience is a co-producer. The social-innovation opportunity is to channel that participatory energy toward making, mending and sharing rather than only buying — maker spaces, school craft programmes and library "costume labs" can turn a consumption spike into a skills-building moment.  
Third — and most urgently — *the circular-fashion problem*. Halloween costumes are a textbook case of fast fashion at its worst: oil-based synthetic outfits, worn once, landfilled within weeks. A multi-billion-dollar one-night industry is an environmental and ethical blind spot hiding in plain sight. Here social innovation has concrete answers: costume-swap libraries, rental models, second-hand marketplaces, pattern-sharing for DIY makes, and "costume amnesty" collection points that feed materials back into reuse.  
**Risks and honest limits.** Two cautions. One, celebrating cross-cultural costumes sits a short step from appropriation; the line between honouring a culture and caricaturing it is real, and licensing by the original creators (as here) helps but does not settle it. Two, "circular" solutions can become greenwashing if rental and resale simply add consumption rather than replace it. The goal is fewer, better-used costumes — not a new category of guilt-free buying.  
**Implications and recommended actions.**

> * **For schools and libraries:** run costume-swap drives and DIY "costume labs" in October; reframe the season as a making-and-sharing event, not only a buying one.  
> * **For retailers and licensors:** offer official rental and take-back programmes, use recycled and mono-material textiles, and share profits or credit with the source culture's creators.  
> * **For local governments:** set up post-Halloween textile collection points and partner with reuse charities so costumes re-enter circulation instead of landfill.  
> * **For families:** borrow, swap or make before buying; a handmade HUNTR/X costume is cheaper, lower-impact, and a better story than a plastic one.

The HUNTR/X wave will pass, but the pattern repeats every October. Turning a predictable consumption spike into an annual rhythm of making, sharing and reusing is exactly the kind of small, repeatable social innovation that compounds.

#### **到處都是 HUNTR/X：《KPop Demon Hunters》萬聖節服裝熱，揭示的文化、消費與循環時尚**

**引言。**萬聖節將至，有一套裝扮正在「吃掉」整個互聯網——來自《KPop Demon Hunters》的 HUNTR/X 造型。光是九月就有超過 20 萬人 Google 搜尋這套服裝；它登上 Pinterest 與 Spirit Halloween 的趨勢榜首，更罕見地連續兩季稱霸。2026 年全美萬聖節消費預估約 135 億美元，Spirit Halloween 更開出破紀錄的 1,575 家門市因應搶購。表面上，這是一則商品行銷新聞；但透過社會創新的視角，它其實是三個故事：關於「誰的文化能成為主流」、關於「粉絲如何共創價值」，以及關於「一次性裝扮背後安靜的環境醜聞」。  
**社會創新的視角。**社會創新通常讓人聯想到居住或健康，而非萬聖節。但文化正是「規範」被塑造之處，而規範正是社會變革的原料。這波服裝熱之所以重要，有三個原因。  
其一，*「代表性」本身就是一種創新*。一個根植於韓國流行文化的作品，竟成為美國孩童（甚至寵物）的預設裝扮——評論者指出，亞裔（AAPI）文化如今正領銜主流萬聖節趨勢。當任何背景的孩子選擇扮成 Rumi 或 Zoey，一種文化位階就在悄悄鬆動。讓過去被邊緣化的文化變得「理所當然」的媒體內容，從「新規範擴散」的意義上，正是一種社會創新。  
其二，*參與式、粉絲驅動的價值*。這股熱潮並非由上而下製造，而是由粉絲自製版本、教學與二創放大。這正是創作者經濟的邏輯：觀眾同時是共同生產者。社會創新的機會在於——把這股參與能量導向「製作、修補與共享」，而不只是「購買」。自造者空間、校園手作課程、圖書館的「服裝工作坊」，都能把一次消費高峰轉化為培養技能的時刻。  
其三，也最迫切的，*循環時尚的課題*。萬聖節服裝是快時尚最糟一面的教科書案例：以石化原料製成的合成布料，往往只穿一次，數週內就進了垃圾掩埋場。一個規模數十億美元、卻只為「一夜」的產業，是一個明晃晃卻被忽視的環境與倫理盲點。在此，社會創新有具體解方：服裝交換圖書館、租賃模式、二手市集、DIY 版型共享，以及把材料導回再利用的「服裝回收點」。  
**風險與誠實的限制。**兩點提醒。第一，讚頌跨文化裝扮，與「文化挪用」往往只有一線之隔；「致敬」與「醜化」之間的界線真實存在，由原創者正式授權（如本案）有助益，但不能一勞永逸地解決。第二，「循環」方案若只是「新增」消費而非「取代」消費，就可能淪為漂綠。目標是「更少、更常被使用」的服裝，而非創造一種「無罪惡感的新購物類別」。  
**啟示與可行行動建議。**

> * **給學校與圖書館：**在十月舉辦服裝交換活動與 DIY「服裝工作坊」，把節慶重新定義為「製作與共享」而非只是「購買」的事件。  
> * **給零售商與授權方：**推出官方租賃與回收計畫，使用可回收與單一材質布料，並與來源文化的創作者分享利潤或署名。  
> * **給地方政府：**設置萬聖節後的紡織品回收點，與再利用公益團體合作，讓服裝重回循環而非進入掩埋場。  
> * **給家庭：**先借、先換、先自己做，再考慮買；一套手作的 HUNTR/X 更便宜、環境衝擊更低，也比塑膠成衣更有故事。

HUNTR/X 的熱潮終會退去，但這個模式每年十月都會重演。把一個可預期的消費高峰，轉化為每年「製作、共享、再利用」的節奏，正是那種小而可重複、且會持續複利的社會創新。

**來源連結：** [Employee Benefits（Lambeth Council，2026-10-06）](https://www.employeebenefits.co.uk/benefits-strategy/2026/10/06/lambeth-council-to-review-possible-four-day-working-week-trial/)｜ [ABC News（Murrumbidgee Council）](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-08-03/murrumbidgee-council-four-day-working-week/106975192)｜ [South London News](https://southlondonnews.co.uk/local/lambeth/lambeth-council/lambeth-council-to-launch-londons-first-four-day-week-trial-london-2026/)

### **English Draft**

#### **Four Days, Full Pay: How Local Governments Are Turning the Four-Day Week Into a Public Social-Innovation Lab**

**Introduction.** The four-day week is no longer a start-up perk or a think-tank thought experiment. This week, London's Lambeth Council confirmed it is reviewing a four-day working week trial — potentially the capital's first in local government — arguing a pilot could cut sickness, lift productivity and reduce costly staff turnover, with neighbouring Hackney and Lewisham reportedly watching closely. Half a world away, Australia's Murrumbidgee Council has already voted to run a Monday-to-Thursday compressed-week trial from January, an Australian local-government first, backed by 75% of staff. When public institutions — not just agile tech firms — start redesigning the working week, a lifestyle trend becomes a genuine question of social organisation.  
**The social-innovation lens.** Social innovation is about new ways of meeting social needs that are better than the status quo and that build new relationships and capabilities. The four-day week, properly understood, is exactly that. It is not simply "less work"; the credible versions (the "100-80-100" model: 100% pay, 80% time, 100% output) are a bet that we can redesign *how* work is done — cutting low-value meetings, automating drudgery, sharpening focus — so that wellbeing and productivity rise together. That reframing, from hours-as-input to outcomes-as-measure, is the innovation.  
Why does it matter that *councils* are leading? Three reasons. First, *the public sector as a living lab*. Local governments are large, diverse employers delivering essential services; if they can protect service quality while compressing the week, the proof is far more transferable than a software firm's. Second, *equity*. Four-day weeks in the private sector risk becoming a privilege of knowledge workers. Public pilots can extend the benefit to care workers, clerks and refuse crews — and surface the hard design problems (shift work, continuous services) that the hype usually ignores. Third, *the wellbeing economy*. Councils framing the policy around sickness, retention and recruitment are implicitly treating time as public infrastructure for health and care — a quiet redefinition of what public employers are for.  
Crucially, these are being run as *trials with measurement*: Murrumbidgee's 15-month agreement includes a 12-month review; Lambeth's officers are drafting implementation proposals before committing. This experimental, evidence-first posture — pilot, measure, adjust, scale or stop — is the methodological heart of good social innovation. It treats the working week as a hypothesis, not a dogma.  
**Risks and honest limits.** The four-day week is not universally portable. Continuous and emergency services (water, childcare, aged care, animal control) cannot simply close on Fridays; Murrumbidgee had to guarantee these would continue, and residents worried about Friday access. Compressed four-day schedules can intensify long days to the point of harming the very wellbeing they promise. And productivity gains measured in a motivated pilot may fade or fail to generalise. Honest pilots must publish null and negative results, not just the glowing ones.  
**Implications and recommended actions.**

> * **For public employers:** run genuine 100-80-100 trials with pre-registered metrics (sickness, turnover, service quality, resident satisfaction) and an independent evaluator; commit to publishing whatever you find.  
> * **For unions and worker groups:** push for reduced hours without work intensification, and for frontline and shift workers to be included, not left behind.  
> * **For national policymakers:** fund a shared evaluation framework so dozens of local trials become one comparable evidence base rather than scattered anecdotes.  
> * **For employers everywhere:** start by auditing where time is wasted; most of the four-day week's gains come from redesigning work, and that work is worth doing even if you never cut a day.

Whether or not Lambeth and Murrumbidgee ultimately adopt the shorter week, they are modelling the right method: treat the organisation of everyday life as something we can redesign, test in public, and improve. That posture — not the specific four days — is the social innovation worth copying.

#### **四天上班、全薪不變：地方政府如何把「四天工作週」變成公共的社會創新實驗室**

**引言。**四天工作週早已不是新創公司的福利噱頭，也不再只是智庫的思想實驗。本週，倫敦的 Lambeth 區議會確認正評估四天工作週試行方案——很可能是倫敦地方政府的首例——理由是試點可望降低病假、提升生產力並減少昂貴的人才流失，鄰近的 Hackney 與 Lewisham 也傳出密切關注。地球另一端，澳洲的 Murrumbidgee 郡議會已投票通過，自一月起試行週一至週四的壓縮工時制，成為澳洲地方政府首例，並獲 75% 員工支持。當「公共機構」——而不只是敏捷的科技公司——開始重新設計工作週，一個生活風潮就升級成關於社會組織方式的嚴肅問題。  
**社會創新的視角。**社會創新，指的是以優於現狀的「新方式」滿足社會需求，並在過程中建立新的關係與能力。正確理解的四天工作週，正是如此。它不是單純「少工作」；真正可信的版本（「100-80-100」模式：薪資 100%、工時 80%、產出 100%）是一場賭注——賭我們能重新設計工作「如何完成」：砍掉低價值會議、自動化瑣事、聚焦真正重要的事——讓福祉與生產力同時上升。這種從「以工時為投入」到「以成果為衡量」的重新框架，正是創新所在。  
為什麼由「議會／地方政府」領頭特別重要？有三個理由。其一，*公共部門作為「活實驗室」*。地方政府是龐大且多元的雇主，提供必要的公共服務；若它們能在壓縮工時的同時維持服務品質，其示範效果遠比一家軟體公司更具可移轉性。其二，*公平性*。私部門的四天工作週有淪為知識工作者專屬特權之虞；公共試點則能把好處延伸到照服員、行政人員與清潔隊，並逼出那些被炒作忽略的困難設計問題（輪班、連續性服務）。其三，*福祉經濟*。當議會以「病假、留才、招募」為政策框架，等於把「時間」視為維護健康與照顧的公共基礎設施——這是對「公共雇主究竟為何存在」的一次安靜的重新定義。  
關鍵在於，這些都是「有量測的試行」：Murrumbidgee 的 15 個月協議內含 12 個月的檢討；Lambeth 的官員則在正式承諾前先研擬實施方案。這種「先試點、再量測、再調整，然後擴大或喊停」的實驗、證據優先姿態，正是優質社會創新的方法核心。它把工作週當成一個「待驗證的假設」，而非不可動搖的教條。  
**風險與誠實的限制。**四天工作週並非放諸四海皆準。連續性與緊急服務（供水、托育、長照、動物管制）無法週五一關了事；Murrumbidgee 就必須保證這些服務不中斷，居民也擔心週五無法洽公。壓縮式的四天班表，可能把單日拉得過長，反而傷害它所承諾的福祉。而在一個「高動機試點」中量得的生產力提升，也可能消退或無法外推。誠實的試點，必須連同「無效與負面結果」一併公布，而不是只報喜。  
**啟示與可行行動建議。**

> * **給公共雇主：**執行貨真價實的「100-80-100」試行，事先登錄衡量指標（病假、流動率、服務品質、居民滿意度），並委由獨立單位評估；承諾無論結果好壞都公開。  
> * **給工會與勞工團體：**爭取「縮時而不加壓」，並確保第一線與輪班勞工被納入，而非被遺落在後。  
> * **給國家層級政策制定者：**資助一套共用的評估框架，讓數十個地方試點匯聚成一個「可比較的證據庫」，而非零散的軼事。  
> * **給所有雇主：**先從盤點「時間浪費在哪裡」開始；四天工作週的多數效益來自「重新設計工作」，而這件事即使你永遠不真的少放一天假，也值得去做。

無論 Lambeth 與 Murrumbidgee 最終是否採行縮短工時，它們都示範了正確的方法：把「日常生活的組織方式」當成可以重新設計、在公眾面前測試、並持續改進的東西。值得複製的社會創新，是這種「姿態」，而非那特定的「四天」本身。
