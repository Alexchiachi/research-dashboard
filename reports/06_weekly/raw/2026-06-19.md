**熱點一（食）：Extreme Budget Eating — 極限省錢飲食**

**來源 Source：** The Food Institute, ["Extreme Budget Eating: The Food Trend Brands Can't Ignore"](https://foodinstitute.com/video/extreme-budget-eating-the-food-trend-brands-cant-ignore/)；補充：Yahoo Lifestyle, ["5 Most Affordable Ways To Eat Well on a Tight Budget in 2026"](https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/articles/5-most-affordable-ways-eat-130704057.html)

### **English Draft**

**Title: Extreme Budget Eating — When Frugality Becomes a Social Innovation Movement**  
*Introduction.* Over the past week, "extreme budget eating" has dominated food feeds on TikTok and Instagram, and The Food Institute has labelled it "the food trend brands can't ignore." As food inflation continues to squeeze household budgets in 2026, a generation of cooks — led by Gen Z — has turned cheap meals, grocery-haul breakdowns and supermarket arbitrage into a mass-participation spectacle. Clips dissecting how to feed a household for a fraction of the usual cost routinely rack up millions of views. What looks at first like a content fad is, on closer inspection, a grassroots response to a structural problem. That makes it fertile ground for a social-innovation reading.  
*The social-innovation angle.* Social innovation describes new practices, relationships and value-distribution models that meet a social need more effectively than existing arrangements. Extreme budget eating qualifies on three counts. First, it reframes scarcity as creativity. Rather than treating a tight food budget as private shame, creators publicly compete to extract maximum nutrition and pleasure from minimal spend — destigmatising frugality and converting a household stressor into a shared, even joyful, design challenge. Second, it builds a peer-to-peer knowledge commons. Recipes, unit-price comparisons, store-by-store deal maps and meal-prep systems are shared open-source, iterated by commenters, and stress-tested in public. This is collective intelligence applied to the most basic of needs. Third, it is quietly rewiring market power. The viral "TikTok To Go" hack — buying bulk catering trays from fast-casual chains and using them as a week's base ingredients — is a form of consumer arbitrage that exploits pricing inefficiencies. Combined with bulk-buying at warehouse clubs and disciplined meal planning, it pressures retailers and brands to compete on transparent value rather than marketing gloss.  
The deeper innovation is social, not culinary. The trend lowers the barrier to food literacy: a teenager with a phone can now absorb budgeting and cooking skills that were previously passed down unevenly within families. It also carries an implicit waste-reduction logic — planning, portioning and "use-it-up" cooking shrink the household food waste that accounts for a large share of edible food thrown away. And it creates weak-tie solidarity: strangers troubleshooting each other's grocery dilemmas form a loose mutual-aid network that, while commercialised, still circulates genuinely useful survival knowledge during a cost-of-living crunch.  
There are tensions worth naming. Algorithmic incentives reward extremity and aesthetics over nutritional adequacy, so "cheapest" can quietly crowd out "healthiest." Brands risk co-opting the movement with "value-washing." And the trend treats a systemic affordability problem as an individual optimisation puzzle, which can let policymakers off the hook.  
*Actionable implications.* For civic and community actors: channel the energy into durable infrastructure — neighbourhood bulk-buying clubs, community fridges, and free "cook-along" workshops that pair the viral format with dietitian input so affordability and nutrition advance together. For retailers and food brands: respond with genuine transparency — clear unit pricing, honest "value tiers," surplus-food markdowns, and creator partnerships that teach rather than merely sell. For local governments and schools: fund practical food-literacy curricula and back social enterprises (surplus-redistribution apps, low-cost meal kits) that institutionalise what the trend improvises. For individuals: form a small buying pool with neighbours, treat shared recipes as a commons to improve and pass on, and use the planning discipline to cut waste. The lesson of extreme budget eating is that constraint, made social and shared, can become a public good — but only if we build lasting structures around the fleeting virality.

**標題：極限省錢飲食——當「節儉」成為一場社會創新運動**  
*引言。*過去一週，「極限省錢飲食（Extreme Budget Eating）」攻佔了 TikTok 與 Instagram 的美食版面，The Food Institute 更直接稱它是「品牌無法忽視的飲食趨勢」。在 2026 年食品通膨持續擠壓家庭預算的背景下，以 Z 世代為首的一群「煮飯人」，把便宜餐點、採買開箱、超市比價變成了全民參與的內容奇觀。教人用零頭預算餵飽全家的影片，動輒累積數百萬觀看。乍看是內容潮流，細看卻是對結構性問題的草根回應——這正是社會創新最好的切入點。  
*社會創新角度。*社會創新指的是：用新的做法、新的關係與新的價值分配模式，比既有制度更有效地回應社會需求。極限省錢飲食在三個層面上符合此定義。其一，它把「匱乏」重新框架為「創意」。創作者不再把吃緊的伙食費視為私下的難堪，而是公開競賽，比誰能用最少的錢榨出最多的營養與快樂——這去除了節儉的污名，把家庭壓力源轉化為共享、甚至帶點樂趣的設計挑戰。其二，它建立了一座點對點的「知識共有財」。食譜、單價比較、各店特價地圖、備餐系統皆以開源方式分享，被留言者反覆迭代、在公開場域接受壓力測試，這是把集體智慧應用在最基本需求上的典範。其三，它正悄悄重寫市場權力。爆紅的「TikTok To Go」訣竅——向速食連鎖購買大份外燴餐盤，當成一週料理的基底食材——是一種利用定價低效的消費套利；再加上量販囤貨與嚴謹的備餐規劃，逼使零售商與品牌必須在「透明的真實價值」而非行銷包裝上競爭。  
更深層的創新是社會性的，而非烹飪上的。這股風潮降低了「食物素養」的門檻：一個帶著手機的青少年，如今能學到過去在家庭內部傳承得並不均勻的預算與烹飪技能。它也內建了減廢邏輯——規劃、分量控制與「把食材用完」的煮法，能縮減家庭食物浪費（可食用食物被丟棄的一大來源）。它更創造了「弱連結式的團結」：素昧平生的人互相解決採買難題，形成一張雖被商業化、卻仍在生活成本緊縮期間流通著實用生存知識的鬆散互助網。  
但也有值得指出的張力。演算法獎勵「極端」與「好看」更甚於「營養充足」，於是「最便宜」可能悄悄排擠掉「最健康」；品牌也可能以「價值漂綠」收編這股運動；而把系統性的可負擔問題當成個人最佳化的解謎遊戲，恐怕也讓政策制定者得以卸責。  
*可行啟示與行動建議。*對公民與社區行動者：把這股能量導向能持久的基礎設施——社區團購、社區共享冰箱、以及把爆紅形式與營養師建議結合的免費「共煮工作坊」，讓可負擔與營養一起前進。對零售與食品品牌：以真正的透明度回應——清楚的單價標示、誠實的「價格分級」、即期品折扣，以及「教學而非純推銷」的創作者合作。對地方政府與學校：投入實用的食物素養課程，並支持把臨時做法制度化的社會企業（剩食媒合 App、平價餐包）。對個人：與鄰居組個小型團購、把共享食譜當成可改良並傳承的共有財、並用規劃紀律來減少浪費。極限省錢飲食給我們的啟示是：當「限制」被社會化、被共享，它能轉化為一種公共財——但前提是，我們要在轉瞬即逝的爆紅之外，建立起能長存的結構。

**熱點二（行）：病毒式社群文化正在重寫旅遊經濟**

**來源 Source：** PR Newswire / Going, ["Viral culture is quietly rewriting the economics of travel, Going's 2026 report finds"](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/viral-culture-is-quietly-rewriting-the-economics-of-travel-goings-2026-report-finds-302634489.html)

### **English Draft**

**Title: When the Algorithm Picks the Destination — Rethinking Viral Travel as Social Innovation**  
*Introduction.* A widely shared finding this week comes from Going's 2026 travel report: viral culture is "quietly rewriting the economics of travel." Social media now shapes the destination choices of roughly a quarter of travellers — and nearly half of Gen Z. A single viral clip can be powerful enough to move airline capacity, shift prices and redirect millions of people toward a once-quiet town. We usually discuss this as a marketing story or an overtourism problem. But the same dynamics, deliberately steered, point toward a genuine social-innovation opportunity in how tourism distributes its benefits and burdens.  
*The social-innovation angle.* The core problem virality exposes is concentration: attention, footfall and money pile onto a handful of "photogenic" hotspots, overwhelming local infrastructure, inflating housing and prices for residents, and degrading the very places people came to see. The social-innovation question is not "how do we stop virality?" — that is neither possible nor desirable — but "how do we redesign the system so that algorithmic attention becomes a tool for fairer distribution rather than destructive concentration?"  
Several emerging practices hint at the answer. First, demand-shaping by design: destination marketing organisations and creators can deliberately seed "dupe destinations" — lesser-known towns offering a similar experience to an overrun icon — spreading visitors across a region and channelling spending into communities that need it. Second, community-governed tourism: when residents co-own the narrative (deciding which sites to promote, capping daily numbers, steering visitors to locally owned businesses), virality becomes a revenue stream the community controls rather than a wave that washes over it. Third, data as a commons: the same first-party booking and search data that powers these reports could be shared with municipalities to forecast surges and pre-position transport, waste services and pricing rules — turning predictive insight into a public planning tool. Fourth, value redistribution mechanisms: tourist levies, dynamic congestion pricing and "visit-and-give" models can convert a slice of viral demand directly into funding for conservation and local services.  
Read this way, viral travel is a textbook social-innovation arena: a powerful new social force (algorithmic attention) is reshaping how a basic human activity is organised, and the open question is whether we let it default to extraction or redesign it for shared value. The risk of inaction is real — resident backlash, "anti-tourism" sentiment, hollowed-out historic centres. But the upside is equally real: virality can put overlooked rural and post-industrial communities on the map almost overnight, something traditional marketing budgets could never buy.  
*Actionable implications.* For destinations and DMOs: stop chasing raw visitor numbers and start managing distribution — actively promote secondary towns, publish "travel responsibly / go in shoulder season" messaging, and co-create campaigns with residents. For platforms and creators: add context, not just spectacle — geotag responsibly, surface lesser-known alternatives, and partner with local businesses so attention converts into local income. For policymakers: deploy tourist levies and congestion tools transparently, ring-fencing revenue for the affected communities, and treat surge data as planning infrastructure. For travellers: treat virality as a starting point, not an itinerary — visit off-peak, choose the "dupe," and spend with locally owned operators. The deeper insight is that attention is now an economic input as real as capital, and like any powerful input it can entrench inequality or be governed toward fairness. Social innovation in travel means choosing the second path on purpose.

**標題：當演算法替你挑選目的地——把「病毒式旅遊」重新想像為社會創新**  
*引言。*本週被廣泛轉傳的一項發現，來自 Going 的 2026 旅遊報告：病毒式社群文化正「悄悄重寫旅遊的經濟學」。社群媒體如今影響著約四分之一旅客的目的地選擇——在 Z 世代更接近一半。一支爆紅短片的威力，足以左右航空公司的運能配置、撼動票價，並把數以百萬計的人導向原本寧靜的小鎮。我們通常把這當成行銷故事或過度旅遊（overtourism）問題來談。但若刻意加以引導，同樣的動力其實指向一個真正的社會創新契機：關於旅遊如何分配它帶來的利益與負擔。  
*社會創新角度。*病毒式傳播暴露的核心問題是「集中」：注意力、人流與金錢全堆疊到少數「好拍」的熱點上，壓垮在地基礎設施、推高居民的房價與物價，並侵蝕人們慕名而來的景致本身。社會創新要問的，不是「我們如何阻止爆紅」——那既不可能也不可取——而是「我們如何重新設計這套系統，讓演算法的注意力成為促進公平分配的工具，而非造成破壞性集中的力量？」  
幾種正在浮現的做法，透露了答案的方向。其一，刻意「塑造需求」：目的地行銷組織與創作者可以有意識地播種「替身目的地（dupe destinations）」——提供與爆滿名景相似體驗的冷門小鎮，把旅客分散到整個區域，把消費導向真正需要的社區。其二，社區治理型旅遊：當居民共同掌握敘事權（決定推廣哪些景點、設定每日人數上限、把旅客導向在地經營的店家），爆紅就從一道淹沒社區的浪，變成一條由社區自己掌控的收入來源。其三，把資料當共有財：驅動這些報告的第一方訂房與搜尋資料，其實可以分享給地方政府，用以預測人潮高峰，預先部署運輸、清潔服務與定價規則，把預測洞察轉化為公共規劃工具。其四，價值再分配機制：觀光稅、動態壅塞定價、「造訪即回饋」模式，能把一部分爆紅需求直接轉成保育與在地服務的經費。  
這樣讀來，病毒式旅遊正是社會創新的經典場域：一股強大的新社會力量（演算法注意力）正在重塑一項基本人類活動的組織方式，而懸而未決的問題是——我們要任由它預設走向「榨取」，還是重新設計它走向「共享價值」？不作為的風險是真實的：居民反彈、「反觀光」情緒、被掏空的歷史街區。但好處同樣真實：爆紅能讓被忽視的鄉村與後工業社區幾乎一夜登上版圖，這是傳統行銷預算買不到的。  
*可行啟示與行動建議。*對目的地與行銷組織：別再追逐「總人次」，改為管理「分布」——主動推廣次級城鎮、發布「負責任旅遊／淡季再去」的訊息，並與居民共創行銷。對平台與創作者：提供脈絡而非僅有奇觀——負責任地標註地點、主動推薦冷門替代方案、與在地店家合作，讓注意力轉化為在地收入。對政策制定者：透明地運用觀光稅與壅塞工具，把收入專款專用於受影響社區，並把人潮資料視為規劃基礎設施。對旅客：把爆紅當成起點而非行程——避開尖峰、選擇「替身景點」、把錢花在在地經營者身上。更深層的洞見是：注意力如今已是與資本同樣真實的經濟投入，而像任何強大的投入一樣，它可以鞏固不平等，也可以被治理向公平。旅遊的社會創新，就是「刻意」選擇後者。

**熱點三（住）：Salvaged Stays 與農場住宿——2026 旅宿新趨勢**

**來源 Source：** Expedia / Hotels.com / Vrbo "Unpack '26"，經 [getBoat](https://blog.getboat.com/news/exploring-travel-trends-2026/)、[Parade](https://parade.com/travel/salvaged-stays-newest-hotel-trend) 與 [Expedia Newsroom](https://www.expedia.com/newsroom/unpack26-canada) 報導

### **English Draft**

**Title: Sleeping in a Former Prison — How "Salvaged Stays" Turn Heritage into Shared Value**  
*Introduction.* This week's travel-trend conversation has been dominated by Expedia, Hotels.com and Vrbo's "Unpack '26" report, drawn from 24,000 travellers across 18 countries. Its breakout finding: "Salvaged Stays." Hotels.com reports a surge in bookings for adaptive-reuse properties — former banks, courthouses, schools, train stations, abbeys and even prisons reborn as design-forward boutique hotels (think Montreal's Hotel Place d'Armes, once a federal building and bank). Alongside this runs a renewed appetite for farm stays and rural escapes. Behind the aesthetic appeal of sleeping inside history lies a striking social-innovation story about land, buildings and community.  
*The social-innovation angle.* At its heart, the salvaged stay is adaptive reuse — repurposing an existing structure rather than demolishing and building new. That is one of the most consequential moves in sustainable development. Construction and demolition generate enormous waste and embodied carbon; reusing a standing building avoids most of that, making the "coolest hotel of 2026" also, quietly, one of the lower-carbon ones. Social innovation here is the recombination of an old asset to serve a new social and economic need without the environmental cost of starting from scratch.  
The community dimension is just as important. Disused civic buildings — a shuttered courthouse, an abandoned station — are often blights that drag down a neighbourhood and erase local memory. Converting them into hospitality venues can anchor regeneration: jobs return, footfall returns, and a building that told a community's story is preserved rather than bulldozed. Done well, this is heritage-led, place-based economic development, with the added twist that the property's history becomes its marketing asset — the prison cell and the bank vault are features, not flaws. Farm stays extend the same logic into rural economies: they diversify farm income, keep working land viable, transmit agricultural knowledge to urban visitors, and redistribute tourist spending away from saturated cities toward the countryside.  
There are caveats. Adaptive reuse can tip into gentrification, pricing out the very residents whose neighbourhood is being "revitalised." Heritage can be aestheticised into a hollow theme — a former prison sold as quirky charm while erasing the harder histories it held. And farm stays only deliver community value if the farmer retains ownership and control, rather than land being bought up by hospitality conglomerates. The innovation is real, but its social outcomes depend on who owns the asset and who captures the value.  
*Actionable implications.* For developers and hospitality operators: prioritise adaptive reuse over new build, retain and interpret a site's full history honestly (including difficult chapters), and hire and source locally so value stays in place. For local governments: use planning incentives, heritage grants and tax tools to make reuse cheaper than demolition, and attach community-benefit conditions — local hiring, affordable-housing offsets — to conversions. For farmers and rural communities: pursue cooperative or farmer-owned stay models so tourism income strengthens, rather than displaces, agriculture; pool marketing through regional networks. For travellers: choose reuse over new-build resorts, ask who owns and benefits from the property, and engage with the genuine history and the working life of the places you stay. The broader lesson is that our existing building stock and farmland are latent social assets. Salvaged stays show that the most innovative thing to do with the past is not to erase it, but to reinhabit it — and to share the value that reinhabitation creates.

**標題：睡在一座前監獄裡——「廢墟改造住宿」如何把文化資產變成共享價值**  
*引言。*本週的旅遊趨勢話題，被 Expedia、Hotels.com 與 Vrbo 的「Unpack '26」報告主導，該報告蒐集了 18 國、2.4 萬名旅客的意見。其中最受矚目的發現，是「廢墟改造住宿（Salvaged Stays）」。Hotels.com 指出，這類「適應性再利用（adaptive reuse）」物業的訂房量大增——昔日的銀行、法院、學校、火車站、修道院，甚至監獄，搖身一變成為設計感十足的精品旅館（例如蒙特婁的 Hotel Place d'Armes，前身正是聯邦辦公樓兼銀行）。與此同時，農場住宿與鄉村度假的需求也再度升溫。在「睡進歷史」的美學魅力背後，其實藏著一則關於土地、建築與社區的精彩社會創新故事。  
*社會創新角度。*廢墟改造住宿的本質，就是「適應性再利用」——重新利用既有建物，而非拆除重建。這是永續發展中最關鍵的一步。營建與拆除會產生龐大的廢棄物與「隱含碳（embodied carbon）」；再利用一棟既有建築能避開其中絕大部分，使得「2026 年最酷的旅館」同時悄悄成為碳排較低的選項之一。此處的社會創新，是把一項舊資產重新組合，以服務新的社會與經濟需求，卻不必付出「從零開始」的環境代價。  
社區面向同樣重要。閒置的公共建築——一座歇業的法院、一個廢棄的車站——往往是拖累街區、抹去地方記憶的毒瘤。把它們改造為旅宿空間，能成為地方再生的支點：工作機會回來了、人流回來了，一棟承載社區故事的建築被保存下來，而非被推土機鏟平。做得好，這就是「以文化資產為導向、以地方為本」的經濟發展，而且還多了一層巧思——物業的歷史本身成了行銷資產：監獄牢房與銀行金庫是賣點，而非缺點。農場住宿則把同樣的邏輯延伸到鄉村經濟：它讓農場收入來源多元化、讓耕作土地得以維繫、把農業知識傳遞給都市訪客，並把觀光消費從飽和的城市重新分配到鄉間。  
但仍有警語。適應性再利用可能滑向「仕紳化（gentrification）」，把那些正被「活化」的街區裡的原住居民給擠走；文化資產可能被美學化成一個空洞的主題——把前監獄當成趣味賣點兜售，卻抹去它所承載的沉重歷史；而農場住宿唯有在農民保有所有權與控制權、而非土地被旅宿集團收購時，才真正帶來社區價值。創新是真的，但它的社會結果，取決於「誰擁有資產、誰攫取價值」。  
*可行啟示與行動建議。*對開發商與旅宿業者：優先選擇適應性再利用而非新建、誠實保留並詮釋場域的完整歷史（包含艱難的篇章）、並在地僱用與在地採購，讓價值留在當地。對地方政府：運用規劃誘因、文資補助與稅務工具，讓「再利用」比「拆除」更便宜，並對改造案附加社區回饋條件——在地僱用、平價住宅補償等。對農民與鄉村社區：採取合作社或農民自有的住宿模式，讓觀光收入強化而非排擠農業；透過區域網絡共同行銷。對旅客：選擇再利用建築而非新建度假村、追問物業由誰擁有、誰受益，並真誠地參與你所投宿之地的真實歷史與勞動生活。更宏觀的啟示是：我們既有的建築存量與農地，都是潛在的社會資產。廢墟改造住宿告訴我們，對待過去最具創新的做法，不是抹除它，而是「重新住進去」——並且分享這份「重新入住」所創造的價值。
